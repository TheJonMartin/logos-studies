> 📜 **Study Theme: Chosen, Redeemed, and Rebuilt — The Indicative Foundation of the Christian Life**
> Ephesians 1–2 is the most comprehensive statement of grace-grounded identity in the Pauline corpus. Chapter 1 declares what God has done in eternity and history: elected before creation, redeemed through blood, sealed with the Spirit, and given the risen Christ as head of all things. Chapter 2 narrates the before-and-after of salvation at both the vertical axis (dead → alive, 2:1–10) and the horizontal axis (far off → near, 2:11–22). Every imperative in the letter will rest on this indicative foundation. Paul spends three full chapters building before he lays a single command. This is intentional architecture: identity before behavior, grace before law, indicative before imperative.

## Ephesians 1:1–2 — Apostle to the Saints: Grace Before Command

*"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, To the saints who are in Ephesus, and are faithful in Christ Jesus: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**apostolos** *(Noun, nominative masculine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "apostle" | NIV: "apostle" | NASB: "apostle"
From *apostellō* (to send out with authority and a specific mission). Paul introduces himself not by credentials he achieved but by a commission he received — "by the will of God" removes any note of self-promotion. The authority behind this letter is entirely derived, not self-generated. For AuDHD believers who struggle with imposter syndrome and the gap between who they feel they are and what they are called to do — Paul's own authority being wholly derived (not earned) is worth sitting with.

**hagiois** *(Adjective used as noun, dative masculine plural)*
Translations: ESV: "saints" | NIV: "God's holy people" | NASB: "saints"
Critically, this is the *dative of address* — the letter is addressed *to* the holy ones before it asks anything of them. The word is positional and relational, not a moral achievement. Believers are addressed as *hagiois* on the basis of their location "in Christ Jesus" — not their behavior. Paul calls them saints before he gives them a single instruction, and he will not reach his first imperative until chapter 4.

**charis** *(Noun, nominative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "grace" | NIV: "grace" | NASB: "grace"
A deliberate transformation of the standard Greek greeting *chairein* (rejoice / greetings) into the theologically weighted *charis* — unearned, unconditional favor. Every Pauline letter opens with *charis* before *eirēnē*. The gospel order is encoded in the salutation: grace is the source, peace is the effect. You do not achieve peace in order to receive grace; grace arrives first and produces peace.

**eirēnē** *(Noun, nominative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "peace" | NIV: "peace" | NASB: "peace"
The LXX translation of Hebrew *shalom* (שָׁלוֹם) — not merely absence of conflict but wholeness, completeness, everything in its right relation. Paul is not wishing them a comfortable feeling; he is pronouncing over them the comprehensive well-being that flows from right standing with God through grace.

Note the grammatical shape of vv.1-2: identity declaration → address of recipients as already-holy → gift pronounced before any command. There is not a single imperative in these verses. The structure of the greeting enacts the theological argument Paul will build across three chapters: indicative before imperative, grace before law, identity before behavior.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

Jamieson, Fausset, and Brown note that Paul's self-description as "apostle by the will of God" — not "by the will of men" (cf. Gal 1:1) — carries weight: his authority over the Ephesian church does not derive from ecclesiastical appointment but from divine commission. The letter's instructions thus carry a weight more permanent than Paul's personal standing.

The designation *hagiois* (saints) is understood by JFB as a corporate, forensic identity: the Ephesian believers are "holy" not because of moral distinction but because they have been set apart by God for his purposes — parallel to Israel as God's *qodesh* (holy nation, Exod 19:6). Holiness is first a status conferred, then a calling embraced. The order matters.

JFB notes that the joint attribution of *charis* and *eirēnē* to both "God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" is an implicit Christological declaration — placing Jesus as co-source of divine gifts alongside the Father presupposes their equality of nature, embedded here in a standard greeting form.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Before Paul asks anything of the Ephesians — before a single command, exhortation, or instruction — he calls them *saints*. Holy ones. This is not naive or aspirational. It is the theological foundation everything else will rest on.

The performance-review posture wants to fast-forward immediately to the commands — it scans every text for the KPIs, the benchmarks, the "so what are you supposed to do?" If you notice your brain doing that here, that is the performance-review posture activating. But Paul makes you sit in identity before behavior. Chapters 1–3 are almost entirely indicative: *you are, you have been, you were chosen, you are sealed*. The imperatives don't arrive until chapter 4. Paul spends three entire chapters building the theological foundation before he lays a single command on it. The architecture is intentional.

Grace before peace is also the gospel's built-in answer to the AuDHD nervous system's perpetual background scan: *did I do enough? did I ruin something? is God disappointed right now?* Paul's opening two words — *charis* (grace), then *eirēnē* (wholeness/peace) — declare the order: the peace you're searching for is downstream of grace already given, not upstream of performance you must achieve. You do not work your way to peace. Grace arrives first. Peace follows.

Also worth sitting with: Paul is writing to a real church — people with conflict (see the marriage and household instructions in chapters 5–6), immaturity (4:14), and persistent sin (4:17–5:7). He knows what he's addressing. And he still opens with "to the saints." The label is not contingent on having it together. It is the identity they carry into their imperfect lives — not a status they graduate into once they get things right.

### 📝 Word Study

**hagiois (ἅγιος) / "saints" / "holy ones"**

**Etymology:** Root *hag-*, carrying the idea of being set apart, consecrated, belonging to a different domain. The semantic core is not moral purity but *relational belonging* — marked as belonging to God rather than to the ordinary world.

**OT/LXX Background:** Translates Hebrew *qodesh* (קֹדֶשׁ) — used of God himself (Isa 6:3), of the tabernacle furnishings (Exod 30:25), and of Israel as a people (Exod 19:6, "a kingdom of priests, a holy nation"). Crucially, Israel's holiness in Exodus 19 is declared *before* the law of Exodus 20. The status precedes the commands.

**NT Range:** Used both for God's intrinsic holiness (Rev 4:8) and for believers' conferred status (1 Cor 1:2, "those sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints"). In Paul it is consistently positional before ethical.

**Here:** The word is a dative of address — declaring their status before saying a word about conduct. Cross-refs: 1 Cor 1:2 (identical opening pattern); 1 Pet 2:9 ("you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation" — corporate identity language prior to any imperative).

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller notes that Paul's salutation encodes the entire letter's argument: the church is addressed as *holy and faithful* before a single exhortation is given, because Paul's method is always to ground imperative in indicative. "You are X" always precedes "therefore do X." The gospel-shaped life flows from identity, not toward it.

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott draws attention to "in Christ Jesus" as the controlling phrase that will dominate the entire letter — he counts over thirty occurrences of "in Christ," "in him," or "in whom" in chapters 1–3 alone. Believers are saints not by virtue of attainment but by virtue of their *location*. Union with Christ is the load-bearing beam of Ephesian theology.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien emphasizes the derived character of Paul's authority — "by the will of God" grounds the letter's content in divine commission, not personal agenda. He also notes that the joint attribution of grace and peace to both Father and Son is an early informal Trinitarian claim embedded in greeting form: a high-Christology move dressed as a pleasantry.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes traces the deliberate adaptation of the conventional Greek *chairein* into the theological *charis*, understanding it as Paul's programmatic reframing of every social greeting into gospel announcement. "Grace is the source; peace is the effect. There can be no true peace with God which is not founded on his grace."

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones opens his treatment of Ephesians with a bold claim: this epistle is the most comprehensive and sublime statement of Christian truth in the NT, and even its greeting presupposes the whole doctrine of salvation. To be greeted as "saints in Christ Jesus" is to be located inside a story that began in eternity and ends in glory — the salutation assumes you know what that means, and the letter unpacks it.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- Paul calls you *saint* before he asks anything of you — does that land as identity you hold, or does your brain immediately flip it into a performance bar you haven't cleared yet?
- Grace before peace is the gospel's word order — is there an area of your life where you're running the sequence backwards, trying to achieve peace first so you can feel worthy of grace?
- Paul knew the Ephesians were imperfect and still opened with "to the saints" — how does it sit with you that the label isn't contingent on having it together?

## Ephesians 1:3–6 — Chosen Before the World Began: Beloved Before Born

*"Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places, even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him. In love he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will, to the praise of his glorious grace, with which he has blessed us in the Beloved." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**eulogētos** *(Verbal adjective, predicate nominative)*
Translations: ESV: "blessed" | NIV: "praise" | NASB: "blessed"
A *berakah* formula — Hebrew-style doxology. God is not "blessed" by our praise in the sense that our praise adds to him; *eulogētos* attributes intrinsic blessedness to God. Verses 3–14 form a single Greek sentence — one breathless, sustained doxology. The appropriate first response to election, adoption, and redemption is not anxiety management but worship.

**eklexamenos** *(Aorist middle participle, nominative masculine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "he chose" | NIV: "he chose" | NASB: "He chose"
Two grammatical features are theologically decisive. First, the **aorist**: completed past action. The choosing is not ongoing, not conditional on today's performance, not under review. It happened — once, completely, irreversibly. Second, the **middle voice**: indicates the subject acts with personal interest, for their own benefit or delight. God chose *for himself* — with personal investment and wholehearted intention. This was not reluctant rescue; it was wholehearted choosing of a specific person.

**proorisas** *(Aorist active participle, nominative masculine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "predestined" | NIV: "predestined" | NASB: "predestined"
From *pro* (before) + *horizō* (to mark out a boundary, determine — the root of "horizon"). To predestine is to mark out in advance the boundary of someone's destination. Aorist again: completed past action. The destination was set before the journey began. And the destination is not merely "heaven in general" — it is *huiothesia* (adoption as sons), a specific relational status with the Father.

**huiothesian** *(Noun, accusative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "adoption as sons" | NIV: "adoption to sonship" | NASB: "adoption as sons"
A legal term from Roman adoption practice. Roman adoption was deliberate, formal, legally binding, and — crucially — permanent. The adopting father could not un-adopt. In some legal contexts adoption was more irrevocable than biological sonship because it required an explicit act of will. Paul applies this legal structure to God's relationship with believers: the Father chose, with full knowledge of what he was getting, to make you a son. The sonship is not on probation.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB notes that vv.3–14 form a single Greek sentence — a doxological torrent — and that v.3's "every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places" is the comprehensive summary of what follows. The "heavenly places" (*en tois epouraniois*) is a realm simultaneously eschatological (the age to come) and present (already inhabited in Christ). Believers do not merely hope for these blessings; they are already positioned "in Christ" in the sphere where these blessings exist.

On election (v.4), JFB draws the OT parallel: as Israel was chosen not because of any virtue she possessed but because of God's sovereign, uncoerced love (Deut 7:7–8), so the church is chosen "before the foundation of the world" — prior to any possibility of merit, prior to any act of faith. The choosing precedes the person who will be chosen.

JFB understands "that we should be holy and blameless" (v.4) as the *telos* of election, not its precondition. Election is not God selecting people who are already holy; it is God selecting people and designating holiness as their destination. The phrase "in love" (v.4-5) modifies the predestination clause: "in love he predestined us" — the emotional register of the eternal decree is not cold sovereignty but warm, personal, choosing love.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Here is the passage that Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria most desperately needs and most reliably misreads. RSD operates on one core assumption: belonging is contingent and can be revoked at any moment. Every perceived failure, every social stumble, every episode of emotional dysregulation updates the internal ledger: *am I still accepted? Did that finally exhaust his patience? Is God's approval still intact?* The RSD brain is a constant, exhausting audit of standing.

Paul's answer in Ephesians 1:3-6 is not "you're doing well enough." It is something structurally different from reassurance. The choosing happened *before the foundation of the world* — before you were born, before you sinned, before you were diagnosed, before your most recent failure, before this week's spiral. Think of it as a timestamp on a file. God's election of you in Christ has a pre-creation timestamp. Today's failure does not alter the timestamp. Today's emotional flood does not alter the timestamp. The RSD lie says belonging is always being re-evaluated. Paul's aorist grammar says otherwise: completed, done, irreversible.

The middle voice of *eklexamenos* is worth sitting with specifically. God chose *for himself*, with personal investment and delight. The middle voice means this was wholehearted, personally interested choosing — not reluctant rescue of an inconvenient person. The RSD brain that fears it was a burden, an imposition, too much — the middle voice pushes back directly: God chose you *for himself*, because he wanted you, with full knowledge of what he was getting.

The purpose of election is also critical and also misread: "that we should be holy and blameless before him." The shame spiral wants to read this as a condition — *you were chosen IF you become holy*. But this is a purpose clause, not a conditional: you were chosen *toward* holiness. Holiness is the destination of the journey, not the ticket for boarding. You don't need to achieve holiness for God to keep you chosen; you were chosen and predestined *because* God determined holiness as where you're going. The direction is set. You are already on the train.

And *huiothesia* — adoption — is the deepest answer to RSD's haunting question: "do I really belong here?" Roman adoption was irreversible by legal design. The adopting father could not un-adopt. Paul applies this structure to God: the Father chose, with full knowledge of what he was getting, to make you a son. Not a probationary son. Not a son on a performance plan. A son — legally, permanently, irrevocably. The adoption is not in danger of being reversed by your worst week.

### 📝 Word Study

**eklexamenos (ἐκλεξάμενος) / "chosen"**

**Etymology:** *Ek* (out of, from among) + *legō* (to pick, select, gather). The compound means to pick out from a group — intentional, deliberate, specific selection. The etymology encodes both the specificity and the intentionality of the act.

**OT/LXX Background:** The *eklektos* word group translates Hebrew *bāhar* (בָּחַר — to choose, select) and is used of God's election of Israel (Isa 43:20; 45:4), of the Servant of YHWH (Isa 42:1, quoted in Matt 12:18), and of Jerusalem (Isa 14:1). The controlling OT logic is Deuteronomy 7:7–8: election is grounded in love, not merit — "not because you were more in number… but because the LORD loves you."

**NT Range:** Used of Christ choosing the Twelve (John 15:16, 19), of God shortening history for the elect (Matt 24:22), and here in the fullest soteriological sense. The Johannine parallel in John 15:16 — "You did not choose me, but I chose you" — is a near-perfect theological parallel.

**Here:** Middle voice (personal delight/interest) + aorist (completed, irreversible). Cross-refs: John 15:16 ("You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you"); Romans 8:29–30 (the "golden chain" — foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, glorification — every link already secured).

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller uses vv.3-6 to dismantle the notion that election is cold or impersonal. The middle voice of *eklexamenos* and the phrase "in love" together show God's choosing is characterized by *personal delight*. Keller: "God looked at you — with full foreknowledge of everything you would ever be and do — and chose you anyway. That is not a distant God executing a cold decree. That is a pursuing Father."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott works carefully through the "in Christ" repetitions in vv.3-6, arguing that the phrase is transformative: to be "in Christ" means Christ's history becomes yours — his election, his death, his resurrection, his standing before the Father. The benefits of Eph 1:3-6 are not given to isolated individuals but to those whose identity is subsumed into Christ's. Election is incorporation into a Person, not a private transaction.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien's most precise contribution is his treatment of *proorisas* — he carefully distinguishes predestination from fatalism by showing that its goal is not merely final destination but *conformity to the image of the Son* (cf. Rom 8:29). Predestination is personal and relational in its goal: not "you will end up in the right place" but "you will end up looking like my Son." The destination is a Person, not a location.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes is precise on "before the foundation of the world" — tracing the formula through John 17:24 and 1 Peter 1:20, he shows it expresses priority over creation itself, not merely temporal sequence. "God did not choose us because he foresaw we would believe; the choosing preceded the corridor in which believing occurs."

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones devotes extended treatment to vv.3-6 with barely contained exhilaration, arguing that the *eulogētos* of v.3 is the *only* emotionally proportionate response to truly grasping what these verses claim. If you have understood that you were chosen before time, adopted by sovereign decree, and destined for holiness by the personal delight of the Father — doxology is the inevitable result. Anxiety and genuine doxology cannot coexist over the same truth for long.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- RSD says belonging is always provisional — does the *before the foundation of the world* timestamp reach the part of your brain that feels perpetually on probation, or does it stay in the "intellectually true but emotionally inaccessible" column?
- "Chosen to be holy and blameless" is a destination, not a boarding requirement — where does your brain flip those two and treat holiness as the condition you must meet rather than the goal you're being carried toward?
- The Roman adoption was irrevocable by legal design, and God chose you knowing exactly what he was getting — what would it actually feel like, in your body and not just your theology, to stop auditing your sonship and just live as a son today?

## Ephesians 1:7–10 — Redemption, Forgiveness, and the Cosmic Re-Integration

*"In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace, which he lavished upon us, in all wisdom and insight making known to us the mystery of his will, according to his purpose, which he set forth in Christ as a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**apolytrōsin** *(Noun, accusative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "redemption" | NIV: "redemption" | NASB: "redemption"
From *lyō* (to loose) + *apo* (away from) + *-tron* (price paid). The word encodes both the mechanism (a price) and the result (release). It is a marketplace and legal term — the purchase of a slave's freedom. Behind it lies the entire Exodus narrative: God "redeeming" Israel from Egypt not through their moral improvement but through divine action and substitutionary sacrifice (the Passover lamb). Redemption is always costly and always personal.

**paraptōmatōn** *(Noun, genitive neuter plural)*
Translations: ESV: "trespasses" | NIV: "sins" | NASB: "trespasses"
From *para* (beside) + *piptō* (to fall). The word means to fall beside the path, to slip off course — not primarily willful rebellion (*hamartia* = missing the mark) or deliberate injustice (*adikia*). It is the language of stumbling, of deviating from the path without necessarily intending to. Paul chose a word that names the texture of failure as *falling*, not as calculated defiance — and forgiveness covers this exactly.

**eperisseusen** *(Aorist active indicative, 3rd person singular)*
Translations: ESV: "lavished" | NIV: "lavished" | NASB: "lavished"
From *perisseuō* — to abound, to overflow, to exceed a container. Used of the twelve basketfuls of leftover bread after the feeding of 5,000 (Matt 14:20). Grace did not merely meet the sin-debt; it overflowed it catastrophically. The aorist indicates a completed past act of excess. This is the direct grammatical refutation of the shame spiral's claim that failure will eventually exhaust the grace supply.

**anakephalaiōsasthai** *(Aorist middle infinitive)*
Translations: ESV: "to unite all things in him" | NIV: "to bring unity to all things" | NASB: "the summing up of all things"
Compound of *ana* (again, up) + *kephalē* (head). To sum up, to bring under one head, to bring to comprehensive recapitulation. Used rhetorically for gathering an argument to its climax. This is the cosmic vision: all fractured, scattered, alienated things — in heaven and on earth — being gathered under the headship of Christ. Redemption is not merely personal rescue; it is the first movement of a universal re-integration of everything sin shattered.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB understands *apolytrōsis* in v.7 as carrying the full weight of both the slave-market metaphor and the Exodus redemption narrative. The forgiveness of trespasses is the specific application of redemption to the legal debt of sin — a charge dismissed, a ledger zeroed. JFB insists that "through his blood" is the mechanism of redemption, not metaphor for mere death: it specifies substitutionary payment, connecting to the Passover lamb and the Day of Atonement.

The grammar of v.8 is significant to JFB: "which he lavished upon us in all wisdom and insight." The *eperisseusen* (lavished) conveys not measured proportionality but overflowing excess — God gave more grace than the sin-debt required. JFB notes that "wisdom and insight" likely modifies both God's act (he acted wisely in the plan of redemption) and the believer's endowment (he grants us wisdom to understand it).

The "mystery" of vv.9-10 receives careful attention: *mystērion* in Paul is not an unsolvable puzzle but a divine secret previously hidden in prior ages, now disclosed in Christ. The content — the *anakephalaiōsis* of all things — is the grandest claim in the letter. Sin shattered the vertical and horizontal relations of creation; Christ is the appointed point of convergence where everything is gathered back together. Redemption is personal rescue and cosmic repair simultaneously.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Notice the specific word Paul chose for "trespasses" — *paraptōmata*. Not *hamartia* (deliberate missing of the mark), not *adikia* (active wrongdoing). *Paraptōma*: to fall beside the path, to slip. It is the language of someone who was walking and fell, not someone who deliberately sprinted in the wrong direction.

This is not minimizing sin — Paul takes sin with full seriousness, and the blood of Christ is the price of redemption for these stumbles. But the word names a specific texture of failure that AuDHD believers know intimately: the fall that happens not from malice but from the gap between intention and execution. You meant to do the right thing. You saw the path. You fell anyway. Your emotional regulation gave out. Your executive function misfired. You said what you didn't intend to say. *Paraptōma*. Paul is naming your exact experience before you could name it yourself — and declaring that this is precisely what full forgiveness covers.

And into this specific category of stumbling, Paul drops *eperisseusen*: lavished. Overflowed. The grace applied to your falling was not doled out reluctantly in proportion to the stumble's severity. It overflowed the deficit. The shame spiral's core logic is: *my failure is finally large enough to exhaust the supply*. Paul's verb is the direct refutation: grace doesn't run on a supply that can be exhausted. It overflowed. There were twelve baskets of leftover grace after your worst day.

The shame spiral also distorts scale. It contracts the universe down to your specific failure as the most important thing in the story: *my stumble is the central event; everything orbits it.* But the *mystērion* of vv.9-10 expands the frame to cosmic proportions. The story Paul is telling is the re-integration of the entire universe under one head. Every fractured relationship, every broken thing in heaven and on earth, is being gathered back toward a single convergence point. Your specific failure is real and required the blood of Christ. But it is happening inside a story whose scope is the universe being made whole. The shame spiral makes your stumble the center of the story. Paul makes Christ the center and puts your stumble, fully forgiven, inside it.

The *anakephalaiōsis* — the summing up of all things — is also profoundly hopeful for the AuDHD experience of living in a world that feels jagged, overstimulating, disconnected, and fractured. Those sensations are not just subjective sensitivity — they are real experiences of a creation that is genuinely broken. Paul is not denying that experience. He is saying: this is exactly what Christ is in the process of fixing. Not just your personal sins, but the entire structure of a shattered world. The jaggedness is real. And it is being healed.

### 📝 Word Study

**apolytrōsis (ἀπολύτρωσις) / "redemption"**

**Etymology:** *Apo* (away from) + *lyō* (to loose, release) + *-tron* (suffix for instrument or price). The compound encodes both the price paid and the freedom secured.

**OT/LXX Background:** Two Hebrew roots: *gā'al* (גָּאַל — the kinsman-redeemer who buys back what was lost, as Boaz for Ruth; Lev 25) and *pādāh* (פָּדָה — to ransom, used of Israel's liberation from Egypt in Deut 7:8). Both roots carry personal, costly, relational liberation.

**NT Range:** Spans present forgiveness (Col 1:14), eschatological liberation at history's end (Luke 21:28), and future redemption of the body (Rom 8:23; Eph 4:30). Cross-refs: Col 1:13-14; Heb 9:15.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller highlights "lavished" as the key pastoral word: grace is not sufficient in a merely adequate sense — it is extravagant. He connects *eperisseusen* to the father in the prodigal son parable, who runs toward the returning son with more than the son needs. "A grace that merely met your need would reveal a different God than the one Paul is describing here."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott is most gripped by the *mystērion* of vv.9-10, arguing that *anakephalaiōsis* is the controlling vision of the entire letter. God's cosmic purpose is the re-integration of a fractured creation under Christ's headship — and the church is the pilot project and preview of that re-integration.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien provides the most thorough treatment of *anakephalaiōsasthai*, tracing the *kephalē* root through biological and political valences. Christ is both the center of gravity pulling scattered elements together and the final endpoint toward which all things converge.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes focuses on "through his blood" as the mechanism of redemption, carefully rejecting the merely moral-influence reading: "A price has been given; a sacrifice offered; an atonement made. The forgiveness flows from the payment, not from the pathos of the dying."

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones reads vv.9-10 as Paul's answer to the question every thinking person must ask: "Is history going anywhere?" The answer: history has a *telos* — Christ as the convergence point of all things — and that purpose is not at risk.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- *Paraptōma* (stumbling beside the path) — does the word "stumble" describe your most persistent failures better than "deliberate rebellion"? What changes in how you bring it to God if you use Paul's word for it?
- Grace *lavished* — not measured, not proportional, not on a supply that gets drawn down — where does your brain's accounting system still insist there's a ceiling you might eventually hit?
- The *anakephalaiōsis* is the re-integration of everything the fall fractured — does zooming out to that cosmic scale feel like your individual struggle is being dismissed, or does it actually help to know the jaggedness you live with is real and is being fixed?

## Ephesians 1:11–14 — Sealed with the Spirit: The Guarantee That Doesn't Expire

*"In him we have obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will, so that we who were the first to hope in Christ might be to the praise of his glory. In him you also, when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed in him, were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it, to the praise of his glory." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**eklērōthēmen** *(Aorist passive indicative, 1st person plural)*
Translations: ESV: "we have obtained an inheritance" | NIV: "we were also chosen" | NASB: "we have obtained an inheritance"
From *klēros* (lot, allotment — casting lots to distribute territory). The passive voice removes the human agent entirely: the inheritance was *given* to us, allotted by divine decision. Aorist: completed, done. This echoes the distribution of Canaan by lot in Numbers 26:55-56 — territory allocated by God's appointment, not achieved by human conquest.

**sphragisthentes** *(Aorist passive participle, nominative masculine plural)*
Translations: ESV: "sealed" | NIV: "marked in him with a seal" | NASB: "sealed"
Aorist passive: you *were sealed* — by another, once, in the past. In the ancient world a seal served three simultaneous functions: (1) **ownership** — this belongs to the one whose seal is on it; (2) **security** — the sealed contents are protected and authenticated; (3) **authentication** — this is genuinely what it claims to be. All three apply to believers: you belong to God, you are secured by God, and you are the real thing.

**arrabōn** *(Noun, nominative masculine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "guarantee" | NIV: "deposit guaranteeing" | NASB: "pledge"
A Semitic/Phoenician commercial loanword — *'eravon* (עֵרָבוֹן) — the earnest money or down payment that legally binds the payer to complete the full transaction. Found extensively in ancient papyri for property purchases and marriage contracts. The deposit partially transfers ownership and legally obligates full payment. The Spirit is not a comforting add-on; the Spirit is God's legally binding first installment on the full eschatological inheritance.

**eis apolytrōsin** *(Prepositional phrase, accusative — purpose/direction)*
Translations: ESV: "until we acquire possession of it" | NIV: "until the redemption of those who are God's possession" | NASB: "to the redemption of God's own possession"
The *arrabōn* is given *toward* the full redemption still to come. The Spirit is the first installment; the glorification of the body at resurrection is the full payment (cf. Rom 8:23). The gap between now and then — the entire span of history — is the period during which the deposit is held, the seal remains intact, and the guarantee is continuously active.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB notes the shift from "we" (vv.11-12, Jewish believers) to "you also" (v.13, Gentile believers) as Paul's deliberate inclusion: the entire economy of election, redemption, and sealing applies equally across ethnic lines. There is no second-tier membership in the family of God.

On the *arrabōn* (guarantee, v.14), JFB connects it to 2 Corinthians 1:22 and 5:5: "He who gave the earnest has pledged himself by that act to complete the transaction. The Spirit in us is God's self-obligating pledge to bring us to the full inheritance." The guarantee is not merely God's promise to act — it is God's legally binding commitment already partially executed.

JFB observes the three-part doxological refrain — "to the praise of his glory" — at the end of each major section: after election (v.6), redemption (v.12), and sealing (v.14). The structure is intentional: each movement of the plan calls forth the same response.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Time blindness makes future promises feel like fiction. "The redemption of God's possession" — the full inheritance still to come — is a future event. And for the ADHD brain, "not yet" has a reliable tendency to collapse into "not real." Paul's answer to this is not "try harder to visualize your glorified future self." His answer is the *arrabōn*: the deposit, the down payment, the guarantee already given and *present right now*. The Spirit you have today is not a lesser gift while you wait for the real thing. The Spirit *is* the eschatological future arriving early in your body.

When you experience the Spirit's presence — the quiet assurance that you are held, the love of God breaking through the noise (Rom 5:5), the inexplicable peace in a hard moment — that is not a pale shadow of glory. That is the age to come showing up ahead of schedule. The deposit is the same material as the full payment, just less of it. You don't need to manufacture feelings about a future you can't feel; you can anchor in the Spirit who is already present as the foretaste of that future.

The *sphragizō* (sealing) addresses RSD with precision. A seal in the ancient world carried three simultaneous meanings: ownership (*you belong to God*), security (*you are protected by God*), and authentication (*you are genuinely what you claim to be*). The RSD brain perpetually challenges all three: "Do I really belong? Am I actually secure? Is my faith even real?" The seal answers all three in a single divine act. And the seal was applied in the aorist passive — it happened once, by God's action, not yours.

### 📝 Word Study

**arrabōn (ἀρραβών) / "guarantee" / "deposit" / "pledge"**

**Etymology:** A Semitic/Phoenician commercial loanword — Hebrew/Aramaic *'eravon* (עֵרָבוֹן) — a pledge left as security for a commitment. Appears in Genesis 38:17-20 where Judah leaves his seal, cord, and staff as *'eravon* with Tamar — collateral that represented him and obligated him to return.

**Commercial Background:** Found extensively in ancient papyri in property sale and marriage contracts: earnest money that (1) transferred partial ownership immediately and (2) legally obligated the payer to complete the full transaction.

**NT Usage:** Only three occurrences, all Pauline, all of the Holy Spirit: Ephesians 1:14, 2 Corinthians 1:22, 2 Corinthians 5:5. Cross-refs: Romans 8:23 ("firstfruits of the Spirit"); 2 Cor 5:5.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller draws out the commercial metaphor pastorally: the *arrabōn* is both a legal commitment and a foretaste. "The Holy Spirit is God's first installment on you. And first installments are the same material as the final payment, just less of it. What you experience of God now is not a lesser category than glory; it is the same thing in smaller measure."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott reads vv.11-14 as the completion of the letter's opening movement, noting that the three-part doxological refrain structures the entire passage as a deliberate trinitarian benediction: the Father elects (v.6), the Son redeems (v.12), the Spirit seals and guarantees (v.14).

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien argues carefully that the seal is the Spirit himself — not merely an action performed by the Spirit. The Spirit is simultaneously the agent and the content of the sealing. This makes the security exceptionally robust: the guarantee is divine personhood, not merely a divine promise.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes traces the *arrabōn* through Roman legal practice: "He who has given the earnest has, by that act, pledged himself to make over the full possession. God has given the Spirit as the pledge and first-fruits of future glory. He is thereby bound by his own act to confer the full inheritance."

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones finds in vv.13-14 the pastoral key to assurance, arguing explicitly against any "second blessing" interpretation: the Spirit's sealing is the Spirit's own witness given at the moment of genuine faith — not an experience to be sought later but the authenticating work present from the beginning of Christian life.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- The Spirit in you right now is the eschatological future arriving early as a down payment — does that feel like a foretaste of something real and coming, or more like a faint signal you're not sure you're reading correctly?
- The seal carries three meanings simultaneously — ownership, security, authentication — which of those three does your RSD brain most frequently challenge, and what would it mean to reckon on that specific one as a legal fact today?
- "To the praise of his glory" appears three times as the telos of election, redemption, and sealing — if even your stumbling, struggling, doubting life participates in that glory, what changes about how you carry the weight of your most persistent failures?

## Ephesians 1:15–17 — Thanksgiving and the Prayer for Wisdom

*"For this reason, because I have heard of your faith in the Lord Jesus and your love toward all the saints, I do not cease to give thanks for you, remembering you in my prayers, that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give you the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**epignōsin** *(Noun, accusative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "knowledge" | NIV: "know him better" | NASB: "knowledge"
The intensified form of *gnōsis* (knowledge): *epi* (upon, intensive prefix) + *gnōsis* = full, thorough, relational knowing — not merely cognitive acquaintance but deep experiential knowledge. Paul prays for something richer than knowing *about* God; he prays for knowing *him*. The AuDHD brain that excels at deep, hyperfocused learning is not disqualified from this knowledge — it is wired for it.

**sophia** *(Noun, genitive feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "wisdom" | NIV: "wisdom" | NASB: "wisdom"
The Greek counterpart to Hebrew *ḥokmāh* (חָכְמָה) — not abstract philosophical reasoning but practical, relational understanding of how to live well in the world under God. Wisdom in the OT is consistently a gift received (Prov 2:6) not an achievement generated. Paul does not exhort the Ephesians to become wiser; he prays that God will *give* them wisdom.

**apokalypseōs** *(Noun, genitive feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "revelation" | NIV: "revelation" | NASB: "revelation"
From *apo* (away from) + *kalyptō* (to cover, to conceal) — the removal of a cover, an unveiling. *Apokalypsis* is not insight generated by human effort; it is divine disclosure of what was previously hidden. The knowledge of God is not produced by the mind; it is received by a spirit oriented toward him.

Note Paul's posture in vv.15-17: he does not issue commands or rebuke deficiencies — he prays. The thanksgiving of v.15-16 and the intercession of v.17 establish that Paul's primary orientation toward the Ephesians is gratitude and petition, not evaluation and correction.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB notes that Paul's thanksgiving in vv.15-16 — "since I heard of your faith in the Lord Jesus and your love toward all the saints" — is characteristically Pauline: he gives thanks not for their doctrinal correctness but for the relational realities of faith (vertical) and love (horizontal).

On the petition of v.17, JFB understands "spirit of wisdom and revelation" as the Holy Spirit working in the believer to produce a disposition of wisdom and a capacity for divine disclosure — making what is already true in the gospel increasingly real, visible, and practically operative in the believer's experience.

JFB connects "the knowledge of him" (*epignōsis autou*) to the entire Pauline theology of knowing God: the goal of Christian life is not primarily behavioral improvement but relational depth — knowing God in an increasingly full and experiential way.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

The neurology-as-disqualifier fear says: "my brain makes real wisdom impossible for me." The AuDHD mind that hyperfocuses and then crashes, that can't maintain the consistent spiritual disciplines that "real" wise Christians apparently manage, that forgets what it just read — this fear concludes that wisdom is for neurotypical people. Paul's prayer dismantles this at its root.

Paul does not exhort the Ephesians to become wiser through disciplined effort. He prays that God will *give* them "a spirit of wisdom and revelation." The agency is entirely on God's side. Wisdom, in Paul's framing, is not the fruit of sustained human effort — it is the result of the Spirit's illuminating work. The AuDHD brain's inability to consistently produce wisdom through its own disciplined effort is not a disqualification; it is simply the human condition that makes the Spirit's giving necessary for everyone.

The word *apokalypsis* (revelation, unveiling) is also significant. Revelation is not achieved by superior reasoning or sustained concentration. It is the removal of a cover — a divine act of disclosure that happens *to* you, not *by* you. The AuDHD mind that struggles to maintain focused attention long enough to "figure out" God through sustained intellectual effort has not been designed by God to succeed through that mechanism anyway. Knowing God is not a research project. It is a relationship in which God is the active revealer.

Paul's posture toward the Ephesians is also worth absorbing: he thanks God for them before he asks anything. For AuDHD believers whose relationship with authority figures is predominantly experienced as evaluation and correction, Paul's stance — I give thanks for what I see in you, then I pray for more of what God can give you — is a picture of how God actually relates to his children. You are not primarily being assessed. You are being prayed for.

### 📝 Word Study

**epignōsis (ἐπίγνωσις) / "full knowledge" / "true knowledge"**

**Etymology:** *Epi* (upon, intensive prefix indicating thoroughness or direction toward an object) + *gnōsis* (knowledge, understanding). The compound moves beyond abstract cognitive awareness toward thorough, directed, relational knowing.

**OT/LXX Background:** Corresponds to Hebrew *da'at* (דַּעַת) — the word used in Genesis 4:1 ("Adam knew Eve") and in Hosea 6:6 ("I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings"). *Da'at* is relational, covenantal, intimate knowledge.

**NT Range:** Paul uses *epignōsis* consistently for the relational, transformative knowledge of God: Col 1:9-10, Phil 1:9, Rom 10:2. Cross-refs: John 17:3 ("this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God"); Phil 3:10 ("that I may know him").

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller notes that Paul's prayer for wisdom and revelation is not a prayer for spectacular experience but for something far more essential: for the truths already given in the gospel to become "real" — experientially vivid, emotionally resonant, practically operative.

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott observes that Paul's thanksgiving for the Ephesians' "faith" and "love" in vv.15-16 is a deliberate echo of the letter's opening. Theology and lived reality correspond. The prayer of v.17 is for this correspondence to deepen.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien argues convincingly that *pneuma* here refers to the Holy Spirit rather than a human disposition. The Spirit himself is the gift Paul prays for — specifically the Spirit in his capacity as illuminator and revealer of divine truth.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes traces "the Father of glory" as a Hebraism meaning "the glorious Father." He also notes that Paul's intercession "ceases not" — the present tense indicates continuous, habitual intercession.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones reads the prayer of vv.15-19 as the necessary sequel to the doctrine of vv.3-14: "The trouble with us is not that we do not know these things theoretically — many of us could repeat them like a catechism. The trouble is that they are not real to us." The prayer is for the gap between knowing and *knowing* to close.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- Wisdom here is something God *gives* through the Spirit, not something you produce through sustained effort — does that reframe what "becoming wiser" looks like for your specific brain and its specific constraints?
- Paul's first move toward the Ephesians is thanksgiving, not evaluation — how would your experience of God shift if you believed his first orientation toward you was gratitude for what he sees, rather than assessment of what you're still lacking?
- *Epignōsis* is relational knowing, not information storage — is there a difference between what you know *about* God and your experience of actually *knowing* him? What would it look like to pray Paul's prayer for yourself today?

## Ephesians 1:18–21 — Eyes of the Heart: Hope, Inheritance, and Resurrection Power

*"having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, that you may know what is the hope to which he has called you, what are the riches of his glorious inheritance in the saints, and what is the immeasurable greatness of his power toward us who believe, according to the working of his great might that he worked in Christ when he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion, and above every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the one to come." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**pephotismenous** *(Perfect passive participle, accusative masculine plural)*
Translations: ESV: "having been enlightened" | NIV: "enlightened" | NASB: "enlightened"
The **perfect tense** is theologically decisive: a completed past action whose effects continue into the present. The enlightening happened — once, by divine initiative — and the resulting state of "having-been-enlightened" is an ongoing condition, not a moment-by-moment achievement. The **passive voice** removes human effort: this is something done *to* believers, not produced *by* them.

**dynamis / energeia / kratos / ischys** *(Four distinct Greek nouns for power — vv.19-20)*
Translations: ESV: "power / working / might / strength" | NIV: "power / working / might / strength" | NASB: "power / working / strength / might"
Paul stacks four distinct Greek power-words in vv.19-20, each with its own nuance: *dynamis* = inherent capacity or potential power (source of "dynamite"); *energeia* = actively working, operational power — power in motion (source of "energy"); *kratos* = might, dominion, the power of ruling force; *ischys* = strength, the capacity to exert force. Paul is not being redundant — he is exhausting the Greek vocabulary for power to make the point that no language is adequate to describe what God has made available to believers.

**hyperanō** *(Adverb/preposition: hyper (above) + anō (upward) — intensive compound)*
Translations: ESV: "far above" | NIV: "far above" | NASB: "far above"
An intensive compound — not merely "above" but "far above," emphatically above. Christ is seated *hyperanō* of every rule, authority, power, dominion, and named name — in this age and the age to come. No power, seen or unseen, human or cosmic, ranks above the risen Christ.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB understands "the eyes of your hearts" as a Hebraism for the deepest faculty of understanding and perception — the heart in Hebrew and Pauline usage is the integrating center of the person (intellect, will, and affection together). For these inner eyes to be "enlightened" is for the whole person's capacity for spiritual perception to be clarified.

On the four power-words in vv.19-20, JFB observes that Paul is straining against the limits of language: he piles up synonyms not to be precise but to be overwhelming. JFB's key insight is that this power is specifically "toward us who believe" — it is directionally operative in ordinary Christian life, not reserved for extraordinary moments.

The resurrection of Christ in v.20 is Paul's anchor and demonstration: if that power raised a physically dead body and gave it life so full it transcended death permanently, then that same quality of power is at work in believers.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

The will/execution gap is one of the most disorienting aspects of AuDHD: you genuinely want to do the right thing, you can see what you should do, and you cannot make the bridge between intention and action work. This gap gets spiritualized: "If I really believed, I would be able to obey. My inability to execute is evidence that my faith is deficient."

Paul's prayer in vv.19-20 is addressed directly to this gap. He does not pray that the Ephesians would have greater willpower or stronger resolve. He prays that they would *know* what power is already at work *toward* them. The four power-words stacked together — *dynamis, energeia, kratos, ischys* — are not describing a power you must access or generate. They are describing a power that is already directionally operative *toward* you (*eis hēmas*). The gap between intention and execution is not bridged by willpower; it is bridged by the same power that raised a dead body.

Think of this in OS terms. The will/execution gap is partly an app-level problem — you know what you want to run, you click the icon, the app doesn't launch. But Paul's prayer is that you would know about the OS-level power that is running beneath all your apps: resurrection power, actively working, not dormant.

The "eyes of the heart" language is also worth sitting with. The *perfect passive* of *pephotismenous* — "having been enlightened" — means this is a completed past action with ongoing effects. You are not trying to get your heart's eyes enlightened; they already have been, by God's act, and the result is a state that continues. When your perception of spiritual reality feels foggy, that is not evidence that your heart's eyes were never enlightened. That is the normal fluctuation of felt experience within a permanent state of having-been-enlightened.

### 📝 Word Study

**energeia (ἐνέργεια) / "working" / "operation" / "active power"**

**Etymology:** *En* (in) + *ergon* (work, deed) — literally "in-working," power that is actively at work, operational rather than dormant. The source of English "energy."

**NT Usage:** *Energeia* and its cognates appear almost exclusively in Paul, and almost always with either God or evil powers as the agent — never merely human effort. God's *energeia* raised Christ (Eph 1:20); the Spirit distributes gifts as he *energeō* (1 Cor 12:11). Cross-refs: Phil 2:13 ("it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure"); Col 1:29.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller finds in the four power-words a pastoral point: Paul is not describing power available to especially gifted believers, but power directionally operative "toward us who believe" — ordinary, struggling, failing believers. "You are not asking God to do something he hasn't already done something far harder than."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott observes that Paul's three "that you may know" clauses correspond to past, future, and present dimensions of salvation: election and calling (past), inheritance (future), and power operative now (present).

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien notes that the exaltation of Christ "far above" every named power (v.21) is the spatial rendering of what the resurrection already accomplished temporally — supremacy in every dimension.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes pays close attention to the phrase "in this age and the one to come" (v.21), understanding it as Paul's comprehensive assertion of Christ's supremacy across all possible time frames. No future age will produce a power that surpasses Christ.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones spends considerable time on the question: why does Paul pray that believers would *know* God's power rather than simply *experience* it more? "The problem is not supply; it is perception."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- The four power-words describe power that is already directionally operative *toward* you — not power you must access by trying harder. Where in your will/execution gap do you most need to reckon on this power rather than generate your own?
- The "eyes of your heart" have *already been enlightened* (perfect passive — done, ongoing) — when your perception of spiritual reality feels foggy, does that feel like evidence the enlightening never happened, or can you hold the permanent state alongside the fluctuating experience?
- Paul prays that you would *know* what power is at work in you, not that you would try harder — what would it change about your daily experience if your primary orientation was learning to perceive what's already present rather than striving to generate what you think is absent?

## Ephesians 1:22–23 — Head Over All: The Church as His Body, His Fullness

*"And he put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church, which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**kephalē** *(Noun, accusative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "head" | NIV: "head" | NASB: "head"
The word carries both anatomical and political-authority valences simultaneously. In the ancient world the head was understood as the source of life and direction for the body — the body derived its identity, motion, and sustenance from its connection to the head. As authority, the head is the supreme ruler, the one under whom all others fall. Both senses operate here: Christ is the source-and-life-giver of the church (anatomical) AND the supreme authority over all things (political).

**plērōma** *(Noun, nominative neuter singular)*
Translations: ESV: "fullness" | NIV: "fullness" | NASB: "fullness"
From *plēroō* (to fill, to complete, to bring to fullness). One of Ephesians' richest terms. Here it describes the church as "the fullness of him who fills all in all" — the church is the *plērōma* of Christ. The church is the sphere in which the cosmic Christ takes bodily expression. The church is not peripheral to Christ; it is his body — the locus of his visible presence and activity in the world.

**ta panta en pasin plēroumenou** *(Present middle/passive participle)*
Translations: ESV: "who fills all in all" | NIV: "who fills everything in every way" | NASB: "who fills all in all"
The present participle *plēroumenou* (filling) indicates ongoing, continuous action: Christ is *continuously filling* all things in every way. The scope is cosmic — *ta panta* (all things), *en pasin* (in all ways/in everything). Christ fills the cosmos; the church is the particular locus where that filling is most concentrated and visible.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB reads v.22 as the crowning application of the resurrection and exaltation: the Christ who has been raised and seated above all things has been given specifically "to the church" as head. The church does not exist as a human institution that Christ oversees from a distance; Christ is given to it.

JFB's treatment of *plērōma* (fullness) is careful: the body is the fullness/completion of the head — the head without a body lacks the instrument through which it acts and expresses itself in the world. The church, filled with Christ's Spirit and gifts, is the means by which the risen Christ extends his presence, speaks his word, and does his work in the world.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Identity vs. behavior confusion often shows up in the AuDHD believer's relationship to the church: "I can't sustain the disciplines, I can't maintain the engagement, I miss things, I say the wrong thing, I'm probably more of a drain on the body than a contribution to it."

Paul's language in vv.22-23 pushes back with a biological metaphor. A body is not made of identical parts performing identical functions at identical rhythms. A body has parts that pulse and parts that rest, parts that are visible and parts that are hidden, parts that operate intensely in short bursts and parts that sustain steady background functions. The body needs all of them. The AuDHD nervous system's different rhythms, intensities, and patterns of engagement are not evidence of bodily malfunction — they may be evidence of being a different kind of part than the ones that tend to be most visible and celebrated in neurotypical church culture.

The *plērōma* language is also striking: the church is Christ's fullness — the sphere in which the cosmic Christ takes visible, embodied expression. The masked version of yourself contributes a performance to the body. The unmasked version — the one that brings your actual intensity, your actual limits, your actual gifts — contributes *you* to the body. The body needs the real part, not the masked version of the part.

Christ is continuously filling all things — *plēroumenou*, present participle, ongoing. The filling is Christ's activity, not yours. You are the vessel; he is the one who fills.

### 📝 Word Study

**plērōma (πλήρωμα) / "fullness"**

**Etymology:** From *plēroō* (to fill, to complete, to fulfill). Can mean: (a) that which fills or completes something else; (b) the state of being full or complete; (c) the full contents of something.

**OT/LXX Background:** In Ps 24:1, "The earth is the LORD's and its fullness" (*plērōma*) — everything that fills and constitutes the earth belongs to God.

**NT Range:** In Colossians, *plērōma* refers to the fullness of deity dwelling in Christ (Col 1:19, 2:9). Cross-refs: Col 2:10; Eph 3:19 ("filled with all the fullness of God"); 1 Pet 2:5 ("like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house").

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller uses vv.22-23 to make the corporate point: the church is not a collection of individuals who happen to gather in the same building; it is a body — organically unified, mutually dependent, deriving its life from a single Head.

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott sees vv.22-23 as the hinge between the letter's opening doctrinal section and everything that follows: because Christ is head over all things and the church is his body, everything Paul will say about Christian behavior, church unity, and spiritual warfare flows from this headship.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien also notes that "who fills all in all" describes Christ's ongoing cosmic activity: the present participle (*plēroumenou*) means Christ is continuously, actively filling all things.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes focuses on "he put all things under his feet" (v.22) as the application of Psalm 8:6 to Christ — a Messianic use that Paul also makes in 1 Corinthians 15:27.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(God's Ultimate Purpose: Ephesians 1:1–23, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones concludes his treatment of Ephesians 1 with an extended reflection on the church as "the fullness of him who fills all in all": "The church is not an institution, not an organization, not even primarily a fellowship — it is a body, and specifically the body of the risen, exalted, cosmic Christ."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- A body needs different kinds of parts operating at different rhythms — where does the AuDHD pattern of your engagement with the church (intense then absent, hyperfocused then depleted) fit within the body metaphor, and does it feel like a malfunction or like being a different kind of part?
- The church is Christ's *plērōma* — his fullness, his visible expression in this place — does that change how you think about your own membership in a local congregation, including the unmasked, inconsistent, real version of your participation?
- Christ is continuously filling all things right now (present participle, ongoing) — you are not waiting for the filling to begin; you are in it. What would it look like to orient your week around perceiving that filling rather than trying to generate spiritual energy you don't have?

## Ephesians 2:1–3 — Dead in Trespasses: The Diagnosis Before the Cure

*"And you were dead in the trespasses and sins in which you once walked, following the course of this world, following the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that is now at work in the sons of disobedience— among whom we all once lived in the passions of our flesh, carrying out the desires of the body and the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, like the rest of mankind." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**nekrous** *(Adjective used as noun, accusative masculine plural)*
Translations: ESV: "dead" | NIV: "dead" | NASB: "dead"
Not "sick," not "weakened," not "struggling" — dead. The Greek word is the same used of Lazarus in the tomb (John 11:44) and of Jesus' body before resurrection (Matt 28:7). Paul's diagnosis is maximally precise and maximally hopeless from the human side. A dead person cannot heal themselves, improve themselves, or will themselves to life.

**aiōna tou kosmou** *(Noun phrases, genitive)*
Translations: ESV: "course of this world" | NIV: "ways of this world" | NASB: "course of this world"
*Aiōn* (age/era) + *kosmos* (world/ordered system) — together they describe the reigning spirit or characteristic direction of the present evil age. To walk "according to the *aiōn* of this *kosmos*" is to be carried along by the current of a world-system that is itself under corrupted spiritual influence.

**thelēmata** *(Noun, accusative neuter plural)*
Translations: ESV: "desires" | NIV: "cravings" | NASB: "desires"
Plural of *thelēma* — typically "will" or "desire." The plural here is striking: not a single ruling desire but a multiplicity of competing desires emanating from "the flesh" (body) and "the mind" (*dianoia* — the faculty of reasoning). The passage locates sinful desire not merely in the body but in the mind — disordered thinking and reasoning, not just physical appetite.

Note the "we all" of v.3 — Paul includes himself in the pre-conversion state. Every person who is now "in Christ" was once in this condition. No one arrives at grace from a position of moral superiority.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB understands the "death" of v.1 as spiritual death — the complete absence of life toward God, the incapacity to respond to divine things. The paradox of being "dead" yet still "walking" (v.2) is the paradox of spiritual death — motion without life, activity without orientation toward God.

The "prince of the power of the air" (v.2) is understood by JFB as a reference to Satan as the spiritual authority behind the current age's characteristic direction — not a dualism, but an acknowledgment that the pre-conversion walk occurs within a spiritually compromised environment.

JFB's treatment of "children of wrath" (v.3) is careful: this describes a condition into which all humans are born, not a divine decree of individual hatred. The inclusion of "we all" in v.3 prevents any reading of this passage as superiority by Paul toward his readers.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

The shame spiral's characteristic move is to take a specific failure, collapse it into a global identity ("I am bad"), and then spiral into paralysis. Paradoxically, vv.1-3 — some of the bleakest verses in Paul — are actually pastoral medicine for the shame spiral, because they offer a *diagnosis* rather than a *verdict on your character*.

Paul's word is *nekrous*: dead. Not deficient, not lazy, not morally inferior to other people. Dead. In the same way you would not blame a corpse for failing to run a race, the pre-conversion state Paul describes is not fundamentally a failure of willpower or moral effort. It is a condition of spiritual death — the absence of the one thing that would make obedience and transformation possible. The solution required resurrection, not self-improvement.

The word *thelēmata* (desires) being located in both the "flesh" *and* the "mind" (v.3) is worth sitting with. Paul does not locate pre-conversion sinfulness only in the body's appetites — he locates it also in the mind's reasoning and orientation. For AuDHD believers whose minds are often experienced as adversarial — generating intrusive thoughts, looping on shame, running commentary that the conscious self does not endorse — there is something real here. The mind's problematic outputs are not a special symptom of AuDHD moral failure; they are the shared condition of every pre- and imperfectly-renewed human mind.

Also important: "we all" (v.3). Paul does not place himself outside this diagnosis. The AuDHD believer who feels perpetually behind, always catching up to the spiritual state they imagine others have naturally achieved — the "we all" is a leveler. You started in the same place as everyone else. You were dead; so were they.

### 📝 Word Study

**nekrous (νεκρός) / "dead"**

**Etymology:** From the Indo-European root *nek-* (death, corpse). Cognate with Latin *neco* (to kill).

**NT Range:** Used of Lazarus (John 11:44), of Jesus' resurrected body (Matt 28:7), and in Paul for both physical death (1 Cor 15) and the spiritual death of sin (Rom 6:11). Cross-refs: Rom 6:13 ("present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life"); Col 2:13.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller uses vv.1-3 to dismantle "the self-help gospel": "Paul does not say you were sick and need a doctor, or misguided and need a teacher, or struggling and need a coach. He says you were dead." Dead people don't improve with better advice; they need resurrection.

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott identifies three overlapping sources of pre-conversion bondage in vv.1-3: the world, the devil, and the flesh. All three sources of bondage exceed individual human capacity to resist — which is why the human solution of willpower never adequately addresses the problem.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien's most precise contribution is his treatment of "children of wrath" — he argues this is an OT idiom indicating that wrath characterizes and belongs to those so described. O'Brien insists on retaining the full force of this language.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes focuses on the spatial and temporal dimensions of vv.1-3, building a picture of total environmental immersion: a person whose entire existence was structured by the world, the devil, and the flesh. Paul's most comprehensive portrait of human spiritual helplessness.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones spends considerable time establishing that the "death" of v.1 is not metaphorical in a weak sense. He insists on the necessity of regeneration: "You cannot make yourself alive. You cannot climb out of death. The first movement must come from outside you."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- "Dead" is a diagnosis, not a verdict on your character — does framing your pre-conversion (and ongoing struggle) as a condition requiring resurrection rather than a failure requiring more effort change anything about the shame you carry?
- Paul locates disorder in both body *and* mind (v.3) — for an AuDHD brain that often feels adversarial and uncontrollable, does it help to know that disordered mental outputs are part of the shared human condition, not a special disqualifier unique to your neurology?
- "We all" — Paul doesn't exempt himself or anyone else from this starting point. Who in your congregation do you imagine started from a better place than you, and what would it mean to actually believe "we all" applies to them too?

## Ephesians 2:4–7 — But God: Made Alive, Raised, and Seated Together with Christ

*"But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ—by grace you have been saved— and raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus, so that in the coming ages he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**ho de theos** *(Nominative phrase — adversative particle + subject)*
Translations: ESV: "But God" | NIV: "But because of his great love" | NASB: "But God"
The two most important words in the passage — and arguably in the letter. The adversative particle *de* (but/however) marks a complete reversal of direction. Everything described in vv.1-3 was moving toward death and wrath; these two words turn the entire trajectory. Paul does not soften the diagnosis of vv.1-3 to make the cure seem more proportionate — he leaves the full weight of "dead" on the table so that "But God" carries its full force.

**synezoōpoiēsen / synēgeiren / synekathisen** *(Three aorist active indicatives — all with syn- prefix)*
Translations: ESV: "made alive together / raised up / seated" | NIV: "made alive / raised up / seated" | NASB: "made alive / raised / seated"
Three verbs, all sharing the prefix *syn-* (together with, in union with). Made alive *together with* Christ. Raised *together with* Christ. Seated *together with* Christ. The repeated *syn-* is not redundancy — it is the theological core: believers do not merely receive the *benefits* of Christ's resurrection and exaltation; they participate in those events through union with him. The aorist tense in all three verbs marks completed past action — these events are done, secured, irreversible.

**hyperballon** *(Present active participle, nominative neuter singular)*
Translations: ESV: "immeasurable" | NIV: "incomparable" | NASB: "surpassing"
From *hyper* (over, beyond) + *ballō* (to throw) — to throw beyond, to exceed, to surpass all measure. A present participle: the riches of grace that will be shown in the coming ages are continuously, perpetually surpassing — they never stop exceeding what can be measured or expressed.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB reads the sequence in v.4 carefully: "rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us." Mercy is the attribute; love is the motivation; the action flows from both. The three *syn-* compound verbs receive JFB's careful attention: believers are not merely the recipients of benefits Christ procured — they are united to Christ in such a way that his resurrection history becomes theirs.

The "coming ages" of v.7 are understood by JFB as the entire span of eternity — Paul envisions the display of God's grace in salvation as an inexhaustible subject of wonder throughout all future ages.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

"But God" is the pivot point of the entire letter and of the AuDHD believer's story. Everything in vv.1-3 — the deadness, the world-current, the flesh-desires — was true and is accurately diagnosed. And then: "But God." For AuDHD believers who have internalized a story of themselves as fundamentally incapable — always behind, always failing, always the one who can't sustain what others sustain — "But God" names the only real hinge in that story.

The three *syn-* compounds address RSD at a structural level. RSD's core fear is that belonging is provisional — that at any moment the relationship can be severed by your failure. The *syn-* language describes union so thorough that your standing is not separable from Christ's standing. You were made alive *in him*, raised *in him*, seated *in him*. His history became your history. For belonging to be revoked, Christ's own resurrection would have to be un-done — which is precisely what cannot happen.

Time blindness normally makes "already seated in heavenly places" feel abstract and irrelevant — it describes a place you can't quite feel or locate. But notice the tense: aorist, completed past. You were made alive (done). You were raised (done). You were seated (done). This is not a future promise about what will happen when you finally arrive at sufficient spiritual maturity. It is a description of what has already occurred to you in Christ.

The "coming ages" of v.7 also address time blindness: the display of grace in your salvation is so inexhaustible that eternity itself is not long enough to finish exhibiting it. The AuDHD believer's story is not too small or too chaotic to be worth telling; it is apparently too rich to be fully told in finite time.

### 📝 Word Study

**eleos (ἔλεος) / "mercy"**

**Etymology:** The standard Greek word for mercy or compassion, particularly in response to misery or suffering. Corresponds to Hebrew *ḥesed* (חֶסֶד) — the covenantal lovingkindness of God — and *raḥamîm* (רַחֲמִים) — the deep, womb-like compassion of a parent for a child.

**OT/LXX Background:** *Eleos* is one of the most theologically loaded words in the LXX, translating *ḥesed* in passages like Psalm 136 and *raḥamîm* in Isaiah 49:15.

**NT Range:** Used in the Magnificat (Luke 1:50), and consistently in Paul as a description of God's character and disposition toward sinners. Cross-refs: Lam 3:22; Titus 3:5.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller calls "But God" the two most important words not just in this passage but in the believer's entire personal history: "Every Christian's biography has this pivot point. There is the 'before' of vv.1-3 and the 'after' of vv.4-7, and the hinge between them is 'But God.'"

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott gives careful attention to the already/not-yet tension in v.6: believers are already seated in heavenly places (aorist — done), yet clearly still living in the present age. This is not contradiction but the characteristic Pauline eschatological overlap.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien notes that the three *syn-* compounds (made alive together, raised together, seated together) are all in the aorist — all completed past events — yet their objects are living believers, not resurrected bodies. The aorist is the grammar of union.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes focuses on "even when we were dead" (v.5) as the precise timing indicator: "The condition of the sinner before conversion is that of a corpse. And God brings life to that corpse. He does not wait for the corpse to improve; he speaks and the dead live."

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones is characteristically emphatic about the "coming ages" of v.7 — the church is not an afterthought; it is the centerpiece of the eternal curriculum.

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- "But God" is the hinge of your entire story — can you identify the specific moment or season where that pivot happened for you, and does naming it as God's initiative (not yours) shift anything about how you hold it?
- The three *syn-* compounds say your status is inseparable from Christ's — made alive, raised, and seated *together with him*. For RSD to revoke your belonging, Christ's own resurrection would have to be undone. Does that reach the part of your brain that still feels the belonging is provisional?
- Your story of "dead, but God, made alive" is apparently so inexhaustibly rich that eternity won't finish displaying it — does the scale of that frame change how you feel about the messiness and inconsistency of your story?

## Ephesians 2:8–10 — God's Masterpiece: Saved by Grace, Created for Good Works

*"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**sesōsmenoi** *(Perfect passive participle, nominative masculine plural)*
Translations: ESV: "you have been saved" | NIV: "you have been saved" | NASB: "you have been saved"
The **perfect tense** again: a completed past action with ongoing present results. You were saved — once, in the past, by grace — and you remain in the state of having-been-saved right now. The passive voice means you are the recipient, not the agent. The salvation is not something you maintain or sustain by ongoing effort; it is a state produced by a past divine act that continues in force.

**touto** *(Demonstrative pronoun, nominative neuter singular)*
Translations: ESV: "this" | NIV: "this" | NASB: "that"
The neuter pronoun refers not to *pistis* (faith, which is feminine) but to the entire preceding clause — the whole complex of "grace-through-faith saved you." This grammar specifically prevents the reading "your faith is the gift" (which would require a feminine pronoun matching *pistis*). Instead, the whole package — the grace, the faith, the salvation — is the gift. No component of it originates in you.

**poiēma** *(Noun, nominative neuter singular)*
Translations: ESV: "workmanship" | NIV: "God's handiwork" | NASB: "workmanship"
From *poieō* (to make, to create) — the word from which "poem" derives. A *poiēma* is a made thing, a work of craftsmanship, an artifact produced by a skilled maker. The same word appears in Romans 1:20 for the created world as God's *poiēma*. You are not a project you are working on; you are a work of art God is making.

**proētoimasen** *(Aorist active indicative, 3rd person singular)*
Translations: ESV: "prepared beforehand" | NIV: "prepared in advance" | NASB: "prepared beforehand"
From *pro* (before) + *hetoimazō* (to prepare, to make ready). The good works were prepared *before* — not defined by us as we go, not generated by our moral effort. This addresses the will/execution gap from an unexpected angle: the good works you are called to are not a blank canvas you must fill through sustained effort; they are a prepared path you walk into.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB on the perfect tense of *sesōsmenoi*: "The present is the result of a past completed act. They were saved — the act belongs to the past; they are saved — the state continues in the present."

On the neuter pronoun *touto* in v.8, JFB agrees with the majority exegetical tradition: the gift is not specifically faith but the entire grace-faith-salvation complex. Salvation is described as totally God's gift.

JFB treats v.10 as the necessary complement to vv.8-9: grace does not produce passive recipients but active workers. The "good works" are the purpose of salvation — though they are not its cause. Not works → salvation; but salvation → works.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Ephesians 2:8-9 is the most direct biblical demolition of the performance-review posture. "Not a result of works, so that no one may boast" — the performance-review posture is specifically, grammatically excluded. The performance-review orientation toward God is precisely what grace is designed to make impossible.

The word *poiēma* (masterpiece/poem) is one of the most significant identity reframes in the letter. You are not a self-improvement project. You are not a partially-assembled product waiting for you to supply the missing components through sufficient effort. You are a work of art being made by the artist who made the universe. The AuDHD experience of feeling perpetually unfinished, perpetually below par, perpetually behind where you "should" be — *poiēma* reframes the question. The question is not "when will I finally be finished?" The question is "what is the artist making?" And the answer is: something he is making intentionally, skillfully, with full knowledge of the material.

The *proētoimasen* (prepared beforehand) addresses the will/execution gap. The good works you are called to walk in were not left for you to conceive, generate, and execute through your own moral creativity and executive function. They were prepared in advance. The AuDHD brain that struggles to initiate when facing a blank canvas may find more traction with a prepared path — something to step into rather than something to generate from scratch.

Identity vs. behavior confusion meets its clearest resolution in these three verses. Vv.8-9 establish identity entirely as gift, entirely outside the performance-evaluation frame. V.10 then locates behavior as the expression of that identity — you were created for good works, as a new creation, which means the works flow from what you are, not toward it. Being → doing, not doing → being.

### 📝 Word Study

**poiēma (ποίημα) / "workmanship" / "masterpiece" / "poem"**

**Etymology:** From *poieō* (to make, create, produce) — the same root as English "poem" through Latin *poema*. A *poiēma* is the result of skilled making — an artifact that bears the mark of its maker's craft and intention.

**OT/LXX Background:** The creation narrative in Genesis uses the language of God making (*poieō* throughout the LXX of Gen 1). Paul reaches back to creation language here: becoming a Christian is a new creation (cf. 2 Cor 5:17).

**NT Usage:** Appears only twice in the NT — here (Eph 2:10) and Romans 1:20. Cross-refs: 2 Cor 5:17; Ps 139:14.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller notes that the "so that no one may boast" clause (v.9) is the stated design of the grace-structure of salvation. "Grace is not merely a nice way to be saved; grace is the *only* way to save people who would otherwise use their salvation as a basis for pride."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott carefully works through vv.8-10's logical structure: neither antinomian nor legalistic. "Good works are not the ground of our salvation but the goal of it. They are not the root but the fruit."

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien argues that the "walk in them" is a genuine imperative — believers are responsible to live out the good works — but the frame surrounding the imperative is entirely grace: the creation is God's act, the works are God's preparation.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes gives careful attention to the neuter demonstrative *touto* (v.8) and also notes the rhetorical effect of "so that no one may boast" — Paul anticipates the objection that even if faith is the instrument of salvation, the act of believing is itself a human contribution. The "so that no one may boast" closes this door explicitly.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones spends considerable homiletical energy on *poiēma*: "You are not God's problem. You are God's poem. There is a difference between a problem and a work of art. A problem you want to be rid of. A work of art you are creating with intention and delight."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- You are God's *poiēma* — his poem, his masterpiece in progress. What would shift if you stopped relating to yourself as a self-improvement project and started relating to yourself as a work being made by someone who knows exactly what he's doing with the material?
- The good works were "prepared beforehand" — they are a path to walk into, not a blank canvas to fill. For the AuDHD brain that struggles to initiate from nothing, does the "prepared path" framing feel different from "you need to figure out and generate what to do"?
- Vv.8-9 specifically design the grace-structure of salvation to eliminate boasting — the performance-review posture is structurally excluded. Where does your brain still try to find a contribution, a component, a way to have some credit in the ledger? What does it cost you to let that go?

## Ephesians 2:11–13 — Once Far Off, Now Brought Near by the Blood of Christ

*"Therefore remember that at one time you Gentiles in the flesh, called 'the uncircumcision' by what is called the circumcision, which is made in the flesh by hands— remember that you were at that time separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world. But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**mnēmoneuete** *(Present active imperative, 2nd person plural)*
Translations: ESV: "remember" | NIV: "remember" | NASB: "remember"
The only imperative in this section — and it is a command to remember the "before" in order to grasp the "but now." The present tense suggests ongoing, habitual remembrance: "keep on remembering." Memory is pastoral and doxological here, not morbid.

**makran / engys** *(Adverbs of spatial distance)*
Translations: ESV: "far off / near" | NIV: "far away / near" | NASB: "far off / near"
The spatial metaphor of distance and proximity is drawn from Isaiah 57:19 (quoted in v.17). The spatial language captures a felt experience: the outsider who does not belong. Brought "near" is the reversal: the one who was outside is now inside — not by their own approach but by the price someone else paid on their behalf.

**nyn de** *(Adversative temporal particle — "but now")*
Translations: ESV: "But now" | NIV: "But now" | NASB: "But now"
The second great reversal formula in this chapter, echoing the "but God" of v.4. "At that time" (v.12) describes the condition of exclusion; "but now" (v.13) announces the reversal.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB reads the list of exclusions in v.12 as comprehensively as possible: "without Christ" (no Messiah), "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel" (outside the covenant community), "strangers to the covenants of promise" (no share in the promises), "having no hope" (no eschatological expectation), "without God in the world" — not intellectual denial but practical estrangement.

The phrase "brought near by the blood of Christ" (v.13) is understood by JFB as the mechanism of access: not by religious effort, not by ethnic identity, but specifically by the blood — the substitutionary death that satisfied the just requirements separating humanity from God.

JFB connects vv.11-13 to the overall argument of chapter 2: Paul is moving from the vertical dimension of salvation (dead → alive, vv.1-10) to the horizontal dimension (far off → near, vv.11-22). Both alienations — from God and from one another — are addressed by the same act: the cross of Christ.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

The experience of being an outsider — of watching from the outside, of sensing that others belong in ways you don't — is a near-universal AuDHD experience. It shows up in classrooms, workplaces, family gatherings, and church. The social cues that come automatically to others require conscious effort and often fail anyway. The result is a chronic, low-level sense of not-quite-belonging that can attach itself to a believer's relationship with God and the church.

Paul's description of the Gentiles' pre-conversion state in v.12 is an astonishing list of exclusions: without Christ, outside the covenant, strangers to the promises, no hope, *without God in the world*. This is outsiderness described at its maximum. And then: "but now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near." The outsider is brought inside not by succeeding at belonging but by the price someone else paid on their behalf.

The imperative "remember" (*mnēmoneuete*) is Paul's pastoral instruction for AuDHD time blindness: *keep on remembering* what you were before and what happened to bring you near. Memory of the "far off" is not morbid wallowing — it is the pastoral maintenance of proportion. The AuDHD brain that can feel perpetually on the outside even after conversion may need this specific instruction: you are not far off anymore. You were brought near. Keep remembering that this is true of you.

### 📝 Word Study

**makran (μακράν) / "far off" — and its reversal engys (ἐγγύς) / "near"**

**OT/LXX Background:** Both words are drawn from Isaiah 57:19 (LXX): "Peace, peace, to the far and to the near, says the LORD, and I will heal him." Paul understands the gospel as the fulfillment of this Isaianic promise.

**NT Range:** *Makran* appears in Luke 15:20 (the father who sees his prodigal son "when he was still a great way off" — the approach is the father's, not the son's). Cross-refs: Isa 57:19; Acts 2:39; Eph 2:17.

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller draws out the pastoral force of Paul's command to "remember" — he argues that the Christian life requires a cultivated discipline of memory. "If you forget how far off you were, you lose the ability to feel how close you now are. The memory of the 'before' is what gives the 'but now' its power to produce gratitude rather than entitlement."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott reads the comprehensive list of exclusions in v.12 as Paul's rhetorical strategy to make the Gentile readers feel the weight of what they were before. The same rhetorical structure of accumulation-then-reversal is present in vv.1-3 → "but God" (v.4) and again here. Paul has a characteristic pastoral grammar.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien notes that "brought near by the blood of Christ" specifies the mechanism with precision: proximity to God was not achieved by religious effort but by substitutionary death. He connects this to the veil-tearing of Mark 15:38.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes is thorough on "without God in the world" (*atheoi en tō kosmō*) — noting that the Gentiles were not without gods (they had many) but without the true and living God, without access to the God who is actually there.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones uses vv.11-13 to address what he considers a failure of gratitude in comfortable Christianity: believers who have never felt the weight of "at that time… having no hope and without God" cannot feel the proportionate weight of "but now… brought near."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- You were brought near by the blood of Christ — not by figuring out the social cues of belonging, not by mastering the right protocol. For the AuDHD brain that has spent decades trying to close the outsider distance by effort, what changes when the nearness is something that was done *to* you?
- Paul commands ongoing remembering of the "far off" state as a pastoral practice — does consciously returning to "at that time… having no hope and without God" feel morbid, or does it actually help you feel the weight of the "but now"?
- The chronic low-level sense of not-quite-belonging that AuDHD often produces — does the blood-of-Christ mechanism of nearness address the root of that, or does it feel like it operates in a different register than the social outsiderness you experience?

## Ephesians 2:14–18 — He Is Our Peace: The Wall Demolished, Access Granted

*"For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility by abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances, that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace, and might reconcile us both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the hostility. And he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near. For through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**autos estin hē eirēnē hēmōn** *(Nominative subject + present indicative "to be" + predicate nominative)*
Translations: ESV: "he himself is our peace" | NIV: "he himself is our peace" | NASB: "he himself is our peace"
Not "he gives us peace" or "he makes peace possible." He *is* our peace. The *eirēnē* (shalom — wholeness, everything in right relation) is a Person, not a product. The emphatic *autos* (himself) intensifies the personal identification: Christ doesn't merely broker or distribute peace; he constitutes it. This means peace is not a state you achieve, a feeling you generate, or a condition you maintain — it is a Person you are already united to.

**to mesotoichon tou phragmou** *(Noun phrase, accusative — "the dividing wall of the partition")*
Translations: ESV: "the dividing wall of hostility" | NIV: "the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility" | NASB: "the barrier of the dividing wall"
A vivid architectural metaphor — possibly evoking the literal stone wall in the Jerusalem temple that separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, where a sign warned Gentiles that entry meant death. Paul says Christ "broke down" (aorist — completed past action) this wall "in his flesh." The instrument of demolition is the incarnation and crucifixion.

**prosagōgē** *(Noun, accusative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "access" | NIV: "access" | NASB: "access"
A technical term from Hellenistic and Roman court protocol: *prosagōgē* is the right of access, specifically the right to be brought into the presence of a sovereign. In royal courts a *prosagōgeus* was the court official who introduced petitioners to the king. Paul uses this word for what Christ has provided: both Jew and Gentile now have *prosagōgē* — access to the Father — "through him, in one Spirit."

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB focuses on the "he himself" (*autos*) of v.14: the peace is not a commodity Christ distributes but his very Person. Christ does not merely stand between God and humanity, he *is* the union of both in his Person (fully divine, fully human), and therefore his incarnation and atonement are the actual substance of the peace, not merely its occasion.

JFB's treatment of the "one new humanity" (*hena kainon anthrōpon*, v.15) is most important: Paul does not describe Christ reconciling Jew and Gentile by making them both Jewish, nor by making them both Gentile, but by creating a third entity — a new humanity that incorporates both through union with Christ.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

Masking exhaustion is the chronic cost of performing neurotypical belonging — scripting social interactions, suppressing stimming, maintaining forced composure, pretending to track a conversation you've lost. The mask is a wall the AuDHD believer maintains to manage the gap between who they are and what the social environment seems to require. The mask keeps you in the room; it doesn't help you belong there.

"He himself is our peace" — not "he helps you generate peace" or "he gives you better tools for managing the anxiety of not belonging." He *is* the peace. Peace is a Person, and that Person is already yours through union with Christ. The belonging you have in the family of God is not dependent on successfully maintaining the mask.

The *prosagōgē* (access) language in v.18 is directly relevant to the AuDHD experience of prayer. Many AuDHD believers carry a low-level anxiety about the "right way" to approach God — the appropriate posture, the correct kind of attention, the sustained focus that real prayer presumably requires. The performance-review posture finds expression in prayer: "I'm not doing this right; God must be frustrated with my scattered, fragmented, inarticulate attempts." But *prosagōgē* is access granted by a court official who has already opened the door. You do not need to find the right protocol — you have been given *prosagōgē*, right of entry, through Christ, by the Spirit.

The "one new humanity" (*hena kainon anthrōpon*, v.15) also speaks to identity confusion. Paul describes the result of the cross as the creation of a new kind of person — not a merger of existing categories but a genuinely new category. The AuDHD believer who has always felt they don't fit cleanly into any existing social or religious category finds something important here: you are not being asked to fit into a pre-existing mold; you are part of a new humanity being made.

### 📝 Word Study

**prosagōgē (προσαγωγή) / "access"**

**Etymology:** From *pros* (toward) + *agō* (to lead, bring). The verbal compound *prosagō* means to bring toward, to lead into the presence of.

**Historical Background:** In Hellenistic royal courts and Roman imperial protocol, a *prosagōgeus* was the official who introduced petitioners into the king's presence. The word appears in Xenophon and later Greek authors for the formal right of access to a powerful person.

**NT Usage:** Appears three times in the NT, all in Paul: here (Eph 2:18), Ephesians 3:12, and Romans 5:2. All three describe the same reality: granted, open, confident access to the Father through Christ. Cross-refs: Eph 3:12 ("boldness and access with confidence through our faith in him"); Heb 4:16 ("let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace").

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller is characteristically pastoral on "he himself is our peace": "The world's peace is circumstantial — it depends on your situation improving. Paul's peace is Personal — it depends on your union with a Person. You can lose circumstantial peace the moment circumstances change. You cannot lose Personal peace unless you lose your union with Christ."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott gives extended treatment to "one new humanity" (*hena kainon anthrōpon*, v.15) — the church as a genuinely new human category. The church is a new creation, a new humanity, that Paul will develop throughout the letter as the "body of Christ" and "dwelling place of God."

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien gives the fullest exegetical treatment of *prosagōgē* (v.18), noting that the access described in this verse is explicitly Trinitarian in structure: access is *through him* (the Son), *in one Spirit* (the Holy Spirit), *to the Father*. Ephesians 2:18 is one of the most explicitly Trinitarian verses in Paul.

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes traces the dividing wall imagery with historical care, noting the physical barrier in the Jerusalem temple and the inscription that warned Gentiles of death for entry.

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones spends considerable time on the structure of v.18 — "through him, in one Spirit, to the Father" — arguing that this is the clearest statement in Paul of the Trinitarian grammar of prayer. "The Spirit prays in you, through Christ, to the Father. Your inarticulate attempts are caught up in this Trinitarian movement."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- He *is* your peace — not he gives you peace if you manage to get quiet enough to receive it. Your peace is as secure as your union with Christ. Where does your brain still look for peace as a feeling to achieve rather than a Person you already have?
- *Prosagōgē* is access already granted — the door is open, the right of entry is yours, through Christ, by the Spirit. What would change about your prayer life if you stopped trying to find the right protocol for approaching God and trusted that the access has already been given?
- The "one new humanity" is a genuinely new category — you're not being asked to fit into a pre-existing mold. Does knowing you are part of a new creation rather than a modified version of something old change how you relate to the ways you don't fit existing Christian culture patterns?

## Ephesians 2:19–22 — Fellow Citizens, Living Stones: Built Together into a Holy Temple

*"So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone, in whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord. In him you also are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit." — ESV*

### 🔍 Greek Exegesis

**sympolitai** *(Noun, nominative masculine plural — syn- compound)*
Translations: ESV: "fellow citizens" | NIV: "fellow citizens" | NASB: "fellow citizens"
Another *syn-* compound — *sym* (together with) + *politēs* (citizen). Not visitors, not temporary residents, not tolerated guests. Citizens — with full civic rights and standing. The contrast in v.19 moves from "strangers and aliens" (*xenoi kai paroikoi* — foreigners who lack citizenship rights) to *sympolitai* (fellow citizens) and then to *oikeioi* (members of the household — family). The progression is from maximum outsiderness to family membership, in a single verse, grounded entirely in what Christ has done.

**akrogōniaion** *(Adjective used as noun, accusative masculine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "cornerstone" | NIV: "chief cornerstone" | NASB: "corner stone"
From *akra* (extremity, peak) + *gōnia* (corner) — the stone at the extreme corner. Architectural significance: in ancient construction, the cornerstone determined the alignment of every other element in the structure. Every wall, every angle, every course of stone was set in relation to this one reference point. Christ as *akrogōniaion* means he is the reference point that determines the shape and alignment of the entire structure being built.

**synarmologoumenē** *(Present passive participle, nominative feminine singular)*
Translations: ESV: "joined together" | NIV: "joined together" | NASB: "being fitted together"
From *syn-* (together) + *harmologeō* (to fit together, to join with precision — related to *harmonia*). A present passive participle: the joining is ongoing (present), it happens to the structure (passive — God is the builder), and it is precise and intentional (the word implies careful fitting, not random assembly). The church is a building under active construction — not a finished edifice but a work in progress being assembled by the divine architect with each piece placed with care.

### 📖 Commentary (JFB)

JFB reads the three-stage progression of v.19 carefully: from "strangers and aliens" → "fellow citizens with the saints" → "members of the household of God." Each stage is more intimate than the last: citizens share a political community; household members share a family. JFB notes that Paul saves the most intimate term for last — *oikeioi tou theou* (members of God's household/family).

JFB finds in the temple imagery of vv.21-22 the letter's most profound ecclesiology: the church is not merely a gathering for worship but a dwelling place of God by the Spirit. What the Jerusalem temple was in spatial, material terms — the locus of God's special presence — the church is in spiritual, corporate terms.

### 🧠 AuDHD Reframe

The word *sympolitai* (fellow citizens) is one of the most direct answers in the NT to the AuDHD experience of chronic social outsiderness. Not "you are now more socially competent." Not "you now understand the cues." Fellow citizens. The same standing, the same rights, the same access as every other member of the community — not by achieving social integration but by being declared a citizen through Christ. And then Paul goes further: *oikeioi tou theou* — family members. Not citizens who live in the same city. Family who live in the same house.

The *synarmologoumenē* (being joined/fitted together) is a present passive participle, which means two things simultaneously. First, passive: this is happening *to* the church; God is the one doing the fitting. You are not responsible for inserting yourself into the structure. The architect places each stone where it belongs; you don't navigate your own placement. Second, present: this is ongoing. Every member currently in the church — including the differently-wired, inconsistently engaging, sometimes-overstimulated, sometimes-absent AuDHD believer — is in the process of being fitted into the structure by the divine architect.

The *akrogōniaion* (cornerstone) sets the alignment for every other stone. Every member's position in the structure is determined by their relation to Christ, not by their similarity to the other stones or their conformity to the dominant pattern of building materials. This means the AuDHD stone — differently textured, differently weighted, differently shaped — is not out of place in the structure. Its position is determined by its relation to the cornerstone, not by its resemblance to the stones beside it.

The temple imagery at the end of v.22 — "a dwelling place for God by the Spirit" — carries a profound implication for masking exhaustion. The Jerusalem temple was not a performance space where God was impressed by human religious effort; it was a dwelling, the place where God actually lived among his people. The church — including the messy, inconsistent, unmasked version of its members — is where God actually dwells. The unmasked, authentic, differently-wired version of you is part of God's actual dwelling place — not the performed, masked version.

### 📝 Word Study

**naos (ναός) / "temple" / "sanctuary"**

**Etymology:** From *naō* (to dwell) — the dwelling-place; specifically the inner sanctuary, the *debir* (הַדְּבִיר) or holy of holies, the innermost chamber where God's presence dwelt.

**OT/LXX Background:** The *naos* is distinguished from the *hieron* (the entire temple complex) — *naos* is specifically the inner sanctuary. The veil (Matt 27:51) that was torn at the crucifixion was the veil of the *naos* — the barrier to the inner sanctuary.

**NT Range:** Jesus uses *naos* when speaking of his body as the temple (John 2:19-21). Paul uses it of the believer's body as the Spirit's temple (1 Cor 6:19) and here of the corporate church. Cross-refs: 1 Cor 3:16-17; 1 Pet 2:5 ("you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house").

### 📚 Additional Commentators

**Keller** *(Ephesians for You, The Good Book Company)*
Keller draws the contrast between v.19's "strangers and aliens" and "fellow citizens and members of the household" with pastoral force: "You have the rights of a citizen and the warmth of a family. Not just legal standing before God but actual belonging in his house."

**Stott** *(The Message of Ephesians, IVP Bible Speaks Today)*
Stott gives extended attention to the present participle *synarmologoumenē* (being joined together, v.21): "The building is not yet complete. History is the building site. Each generation of believers adds more courses to the rising structure." The church's current imperfection and incompleteness are not evidence of failure — they are the condition of a building under active construction.

**O'Brien** *(The Letter to the Ephesians, Pillar NT Commentary, Eerdmans)*
O'Brien provides the most thorough treatment of *akrogōniaion* — he argues for the foundation-cornerstone reading and also notes the connection to Isaiah 28:16 (quoted in 1 Pet 2:6): "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation."

**Barnes** *(Notes on the New Testament: Galatians–Colossians)*
Barnes focuses on the transition from "household" to "temple" within v.22 — the same community that is family is also sanctuary. He finds in this double identity the comprehensive answer to human longing: belonging (family) and significance (being the place where God lives).

**Lloyd-Jones** *(Ephesians: God's Way of Reconciliation, Vol. 2, Banner of Truth)*
Lloyd-Jones closes his treatment of chapter 2 by insisting that vv.19-22 are the practical payoff of everything Paul has argued: "To be a member of the church and to treat any other member as less than a fellow citizen and family member is to deny the very thing Paul has been arguing. The social implications of the gospel are not optional applications; they are the gospel lived out."

### 💭 Pastoral Reflection

- You are a *sympolitai* — a fellow citizen — and beyond that an *oikeios tou theou*, a member of the household. Not a tolerated visitor. Family. Does the felt experience of belonging in your local church match that declaration, and if not, what do you do with the gap?
- The building is being fitted together by the divine architect (*synarmologoumenē*, passive — he is doing the fitting). You are a stone being placed, not a stone navigating its own insertion. How does it change the anxiety of not-fitting to know that placement is the architect's work, not yours?
- The unmasked, authentic version of you is part of God's actual dwelling place — the *naos* is built of real stones, not performance facades. What would it mean for your participation in church community to bring less of the mask and more of the actual stone?
