Ro 6:1–2 "What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin so that grace may increase? May it never be! How shall we who died to sin still live in it?" ▼
α Greek Exegesis ▼
ἐπιμένωμεν τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳG1961 epiménōmen tē hamartia — "continue in sin." The verb ἐπιμένω means to remain, abide, or persist in a place or state. Paul is not asking "should we do a sin?" — he asks "should we take up residence in it?"
ἀπεθάνομενG599 apethanomen — Aorist indicative. "We died." Not a command or aspiration — a declared fact in completed past time. The aorist signals a definitive, historical event that has already occurred. Your death to sin happened when Christ died.
🧠 AuDHD Cognitive Reframe ▼

AuDHD creates a painful loop: I did the thing again → I must be the thing. The brain's dopamine dysregulation makes impulsive actions feel like your true self. Every lapse seems like confirmation of the identity, not contradiction of it.

Paul's opening shot: your identity is not determined by your behavioral residency — it is determined by where you died. You don't live there anymore. The impulse that flares isn't your address; it's a visitor showing up at a house you no longer own.

Keller's clarifying move: The person asking "shall we continue in sin?" has understood grace enough to see its radicality but not enough to see its transformative nature. Grace doesn't lower the bar — it creates, from the inside out, people who no longer want to live under sin's roof. The impossibility Paul argues is not ethical ("you mustn't") but ontological ("you died there").

For the AuDHD shame spiral: The loop "I did it → I am it" is not just psychologically damaging — it is theologically inaccurate. Paul's grammar corrects it: ἀπεθάνομεν is past tense, completed, settled. The recurring behavior is real; what it proves about your identity is not. You are not the thing you keep doing. You are the person who died to it.

Keller
Romans 1–7 for You

The objector has half-understood grace — its radicality — but not its transforming power. Grace doesn't create indifference to holiness; it creates the only sustainable motivation for it: love and gratitude rather than fear and performance. "The logic of the gospel is not freedom from obligation but freedom from bondage to sin." The μὴ γένοιτο is Paul's most emphatic horror-response: the question reveals a misunderstanding of what dying actually means.

Stott
The Message of Romans

The argument turns on the nature of death itself. "If we have died with Christ, we have finished with sin. Death severs all previous relationships." It is not that we should not return to sin; it is that we cannot — in the sense that we cannot remain in a state we have died out of. The moral impossibility flows from the ontological reality. This is Paul's first great "Don't you know?" — a call to apply already-received knowledge.

Yarbrough & Naselli
ESV Expository Commentary: Romans–Galatians

The aorist ἀπεθάνομεν is decisive. Paul does not say "we are dying to sin" or "we ought to die to sin" but "we died" — a completed, historical event grounded in Christ's own death. The question of v. 1 is answered not by ethical argument but by pointing to a fact: the death already occurred. To continue in sin would be to try to inhabit a state one has already vacated. The question reveals a category error, not merely a moral failing.

Barnes
Notes on Romans

Paul's response to the antinomian objection shifts the question from ethics ("should we?") to identity ("who are we now?"). Those who have truly received grace have been constituted as new persons — sin is foreign to the new nature. Barnes stresses that the very ability to receive grace involves a hatred of sin; a person who welcomes forgiveness as license to sin has not understood what sin is or what grace has done.

Merida
Exalting Jesus in Romans

Grace and holiness are not in tension — they are aligned. The gospel is the most powerful motivation for obedience that exists, because it creates obedience from love rather than fear. Merida: "The person who says 'shall we sin more so grace can abound more?' has fundamentally misunderstood both sin and grace." Grace doesn't minimize sin's seriousness; it takes it so seriously that it required the death of the Son to address it.

α Greek Exegesis ▼
εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν The preposition εἰς (into) indicates union, incorporation, immersion into a person — not merely association with Him. Covenantal solidarity language: you are in Him.
καινότητι ζωῆςG2538 kainotēti zōēs — "newness of life." καινός (kainos) means new in quality or kind, not merely new in time (which would be νέος). This is a categorically different mode of existence — not a reset of the old one.
📖 Word Study: καινότης (kainotēs) — DBL Greek #2786 ▼
NT Occurrences: Only 2 — Ro 6:4 and Ro 7:6. Paul is coining or deploying an extremely rare abstract noun to carry weight ordinary vocabulary couldn't bear.

DBL Greek — Two Semantic Domains:

LN 67.101 Newness as temporal — "the state of being relatively recent" (Ro 7:6)
LN 58.70 Newness as qualitative — "a state of being new or different" (Ro 6:4) — Domain 58 = Quality. Categorically other, not recently updated.

The Two NT Occurrences Form a Deliberate Pair:

• Ro 6:4 — "walk in newness of life" (ζωῆς) — resurrection identity, qualitative newness

• Ro 7:6 — "serve in newness of the Spirit…not in oldness of the letter" — Spirit-enabled empowerment

🧠 AuDHD Cognitive Reframe ▼

On εἰς (union): You are not associated with Christ; you are in Him. The AuDHD identity that has been shaped by years of failure feedback does not define the real you — the in-Christ you does. The Greek preposition εἰς carries full covenantal weight: you are incorporated into his death and resurrection, not merely inspired by them.

On καινότης (qualitative newness): The life you are called to walk in is not "a better version of normal." It is a categorically new kind of life — καινός, unprecedented in nature. The AuDHD brain walking in kainotēs is not a defective neurotypical brain. It is a καινός thing entirely. Keller: union with Christ means "you are not trying to become who you are — you already are who Christ made you."

On JFB's "severed our last link": The shame spiral after a setback is, theologically speaking, forgetting — not moral failure but a cognitive failure to reckon accurately. The prescription is remembering, not striving. JFB's phrase "every time we go back to those things whereof we are now ashamed, we belie our resurrection" is not condemnation — it is a call to cognitive accuracy.

The Romans Arc for AuDHD: Ro 6:4 establishes who you are (new creation). Ro 7:6 explains the power you have (the Spirit). Ro 12:2 gives you the daily practice (renew the mind). Identity → Empowerment → Practice. Stott: the resurrection newness is not a goal to attain but a reality to inhabit — "walk in" implies the path is already open.

On v. 5's certainty ("certainly we shall also"): The Greek construction carries guarantee-force. The future resurrection is not wishful thinking but logical necessity grounded in union: if he was raised, you will be raised. For an AuDHD mind accustomed to failed promises and unreliable futures — this is the bedrock certainty: your ending is resurrection, not failure.

Yarbrough & Naselli

The dual tenses in vv. 4-5 are deliberate: we were buried (aorist — past completed) and we might walk (present subjunctive — ongoing possibility). The resurrection life is both already-secured (by union) and actively-walked (by faith and obedience). V. 5's "certainly we shall also" (ἀλλὰ καί) functions as a logical necessity: union in death guarantees union in resurrection. The argument moves from established past to secured future.

What goes into the ground as one thing comes up as another.

Ro 6:6–7 "knowing this, that our old self was crucified with Him, in order that our body of sin might be done away with, so that we would no longer be slaves to sin; for he who has died is freed from sin." ▼
α Greek Exegesis ▼
ὁ παλαιὸς ἡμῶν ἄνθρωπος ho palaios hēmōn anthrōpos — "our old man/self." παλαιός = worn out, obsolete, belonging to a former age. Stands in direct contrast to καινός. The old self is the Adamic self, constituted in sin — not a pre-diagnosis self.
κατηργήθηG2673 katērgēthē — "done away with / rendered inoperative." From katargeō: to render powerless, nullify, make of no effect. Used of abolishing the law (Gal 3:17), destroying death (2 Tim 1:10). Sin's power mechanism has been decommissioned.
δεδικαίωταιG1344 dedikaiōtai — v.7, "freed from sin." Perfect passive of dikaioō — the same verb used for justification. Literally: "he who has died has been justified from sin." This is forensic declaration — not earned by effort.
📜 JFB Commentary ▼

JFB defines "old man": "'our old selves'; that is, all that we were in our old unregenerate condition, before union with Christ" — and corrects the tense: "is — rather, 'was.'"

On "body of sin": "not a figure for 'the mass of sin'; nor the 'material body,' considered as the seat of sin, which it is not; but (as we judge) for 'sin as it dwells in us in our present embodied state, under the law of the fall.'"

On v.7: "literally, 'justified,' 'acquitted,' 'got his discharge from sin.' As death dissolves all claims, so the whole claim of sin, not only to 'reign unto death,' but to keep its victims in sinful bondage, has been discharged once for all…so that he is no longer a 'debtor to the flesh to live after the flesh' (Ro 8:12)."

🧠 AuDHD Cognitive Reframe ▼

On κατηργήθη (decommissioned): The pattern of automatic, compulsive, dysregulated responses has had its power decommissioned — not destroyed yet in experience, but legally stripped of its claim. When you spiral, you are not returning to your true self. You are experiencing the noise of a decommissioned system that no longer has legal authority over you.

On "body of sin" (JFB): The body of sin is sin operating in the embodied condition — NOT the body itself as defective. The AuDHD neurology is not the enemy. Sin operating through the AuDHD neurology is. This distinction prevents the misidentification of your brain as the problem. Your wiring is not the old man; the old man was the Adamic self constituted in sin — and that self was crucified with Christ.

On δεδικαίωται (justified/freed): Sin has "no claim" — including its claim to define you through shame, to maintain bondage, to keep you as its victim. You are not a parolee. You are a fully discharged prisoner — the debt cancelled, not merely deferred. Yarbrough & Naselli: the perfect passive tense means this discharge was accomplished in the past and its results stand permanently in the present.

The indicative/imperative sequence: Notice Paul tells us what happened (v. 6 — was crucified, was done away with) before he tells us what to do (vv. 12-13). For AuDHD minds that default to self-improvement frameworks: obedience is the response to accomplished reality, not the means of achieving it. Merida: "Paul tells us what has happened before telling us what to do. We obey from a position of victory already won."

Keller
Romans 1–7 for You

The old self is not suppressed or improved — it is executed. The language is stronger than reform: "our old self was crucified." Keller stresses the irreversibility: "You can't uncross the past. The old man is dead." This is not a process still in progress but a verdict already rendered. The implications for identity are radical: you are not a reformed version of the old person. You are a new person who shares a name with the old one.

Stott
The Message of Romans

Stott is careful on "body of sin" — it does not mean the physical body is sinful (that would be Gnostic). It means "the body as the instrument or vehicle of sin." The body is redeemable; creation is good; it is sin's dominion over the body that was condemned and broken. This matters pastorally: the body is not the enemy. It is a battlefield that has been claimed for righteousness through the decommissioning of sin's power over it.

Yarbrough & Naselli
ESV Expository Commentary: Romans–Galatians

κατηργήθη (rendered inoperative) — like an engine with its fuel line severed. The mechanism is still present, but it cannot drive. The body of sin hasn't been removed from the believer's experience, but its power source — its claim over the person — has been cut. This explains why sin still tempts: the body is still present. But it also explains why sin cannot compel: its authority has been stripped. The distinction between presence and power is the key pastoral insight of v. 6.

Barnes
Notes on Romans

Barnes applies the Roman legal principle: when a person died, all outstanding debts, contracts, and legal claims were dissolved. Paul draws on this common cultural knowledge to make the point vivid. The believer who died with Christ is no longer liable under sin's claim — not because the debt was forgiven (though it was) but because the person who owed it no longer legally exists. The new person in Christ begins with a clean legal slate.

Merida
Exalting Jesus in Romans

The indicative always precedes the imperative in Paul's theology. He establishes the accomplished fact (crucified, done away with, freed) before issuing any command. "We don't obey in order to be freed — we obey because we are freed." This sequence is not incidental; it is the structural logic of the whole chapter. Grace empowers what it commands by first declaring what has been done.

🧠 AuDHD Cognitive Reframe ▼

λογίζεσθε is the biblical cognitive reframing assignment. Not wishful thinking or toxic positivity — accurate theological accounting. For AuDHD, emotional dysregulation and RSD produce rapid-fire narratives that feel true but aren't. Paul's prescription: identify the accurate thought and practice returning to it. The present imperative means this is an ongoing, daily, repeated practice — not a one-time realization.

The difference from secular CBT: the content — "I am dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus" — is not a self-generated affirmation. It is a declared theological reality. You are not manufacturing confidence; you are reckoning with fact. The accounting term (logizomai) is appropriate: you are entering a ledger entry that God's own record already shows.

JFB's framing: "even as your Lord Himself." Jesus lived in conscious awareness of his relation to the Father. The logizomai calls us to the same conscious alignment with reality. This is not performance — it is communion with what is actually true.

The ἐφάπαξ (once for all) anchor: Christ's death to sin was "once for all" (v. 10) — it cannot be undone, repeated, or improved. Your reckoning is not hoping it's true but aligning with what has been settled. Stott: λογίζεσθε is "the only imperative in vv. 1–11 — all the rest are indicatives." Everything Paul has declared becomes the basis for this one command: think accurately about who you now are.

α Greek Exegesis ▼
ὅπλαG3696 hopla — "instruments" — but the word actually means weapons / military equipment. "Present your members as weapons of righteousness." Your attention, intensity, hyperfocus, body — these are armaments in God's service.

🧠 AuDHD Cognitive Reframe ▼

The ὅπλα (weapons) language reframes AuDHD entirely. The traits that feel like liabilities — hyperfocus, intensity, sensitivity, divergent thinking — are weapons Paul says belong presented to God as instruments of righteousness. Not managed, not suppressed, not apologized for — deployed.

AuDHD often means experiencing your own mind as the enemy. Paul recasts the body, the members, the capacity — even the dysregulated ones — as weaponry that has been liberated from one commander (sin) and is now available to another (God). You are not a broken soldier. You are a liberated one whose armaments are now in the right hands.

On "sin shall NOT be master" (v. 14): This is a declarative promise, not a conditional warning. The future indicative (κυριεύσει + οὐ) makes an unconditional statement about sin's present inability to lord it over the believer under grace. Stott: "Under grace" is not a statement of permissiveness — it is a statement of power. Law could command but could not enable; grace both commands and supplies the capacity to obey.

For RSD (Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria): The intense shame and self-condemnation that floods in after a failure is sin attempting to reassert mastery through accusation. Paul's declaration — "sin shall not be master over you" — applies to shame's tyranny, not just behavioral patterns. The shame spiral that says "you'll never change" is operating against declared gospel truth.

νόμος × 4 Paul uses νόμος (law) four times in vv. 22-23 with four distinct referents: (1) the law of God (the moral/revealed law); (2) the law of my mind (the renewed will's consistent allegiance to God's law); (3) a different law in my members (the sinful principle operating with law-like consistency in the flesh); (4) the law of sin (the same as #3, named for its master). Paul's point: both the renewed nature and the sinful principle operate with the force and consistency of a law.

Not existential despair or defeat — this is the cry of someone who has been fighting hard and is exhausted from the engagement. The wretched man is not one who has given up; he is one who cannot stop fighting.
χάρις τῷ θεῷ charis tō theō — grace/thanks to God (v. 25). Literally "grace to God" — not merely gratitude but the acknowledgment that the deliverance itself is in the χάρις domain. Ro 8:1–4 "Therefore there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set you free from the law of sin and of death. For what the Law could not do, weak as it was through the flesh, God did: sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and as an offering for sin, He condemned sin in the flesh, so that the requirement of the Law might be fulfilled in us, who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit." ▼
α Greek Exegesis ▼
κατάκριμα katákrima Condemnation — not merely the verdict but the full penal consequence. The prefix kata- intensifies: a "down-verdict" that crushes. Paul uses this word only in Romans (5:16, 5:18, 8:1). Its absence "now" (νῦν) points to a definitive, already-accomplished status change.
νόμος τοῦ πνεύματος "Law of the Spirit of life" — a counter-law that operates on the same battlefield as the law of sin and death. Paul uses nomos here in a dynamic sense: the Spirit's governing principle operates like a law — consistent, reliable, overriding.
ὁμοίωμα σαρκὸς ἁμαρτίας "Likeness of sinful flesh" — Christ came in the same human form as fallen humanity (homoiōma, likeness/resemblance) but without being sinful. This precision matters: full identification with our condition, without contamination by it.
δικαίωμα dikaiōma The righteous requirement/ordinance of the Law. What the Law demanded but could not produce in us, God accomplished by condemning sin in the flesh — so that the Law's righteous requirement might be fulfilled in us (passive: God does it in us, through the Spirit).
📖 JFB Commentary ▼

JFB on v.1: The word "therefore" marks the transition from Rom. 7's conflict to "the glorious liberty" here. "There is therefore now no condemnation" — the word is very strong: no penal consequence at all. "To them who are in Christ Jesus" — the union with Christ is the ground of the liberation.

On v.2: "The law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death" — two antagonistic principles are here called laws because of the regularity of their operation. The Spirit's law of life, operating in the believer, overpowers and liberates from the law of sin.

On v.3: What the law could not do — not because of any defect in the law itself, but because of the weakness of the flesh — God did by sending His Son in the likeness of sinful flesh. The "likeness" is carefully expressed: Christ took our nature truly, but not our sinfulness. He condemned sin in the flesh — executed judicial sentence upon it in the very nature that had been its seat and instrument.

On v.4: The grand design of the whole: that the dikaiōma (righteous requirement) of the law might be fulfilled in us — not by us in our own strength, but accomplished in us by the indwelling Spirit. Those who "walk not after the flesh but after the Spirit" — the walk describes the habitual direction, not sinless perfection.

Paul's answer is structural, not sentimental. The five verbs of vv.29-30 are all aorist — grammatically, they describe completed actions. And "all things work together for good" — panta, everything, including the ADHD tax, the autistic burnout, the failed attempts, the things you did that you regret. Not that these are good in themselves but that God is actively, continuously working them together toward a good that is defined as conformity to Christ's image. Your character development through difficulty is not accident. It is God working the purpose of the chain.

BENGEL's comment on the whole: "We can no farther go, think, wish." This is the ceiling — and it's high enough.

Keller on what "good" means: The "good" of v.28 is defined by v.29 — "conformed to the image of His Son." Keller: "Paul is not promising that God will give you a comfortable life. He is promising that God will use everything — including the hardest things — to make you more like Jesus. That is the good He is working all things toward." This redefines the promise entirely. The AuDHD experience of difficulty, failure, and difference is not a detour from the good God is working; it is the material He is using to produce the specific good He has in mind: Christ-likeness.

The security of the unbroken chain: Stott on the five aorists: "All five verbs describe God's sovereign action. All five are stated in the past tense — including glorification, which has not yet happened historically. Paul states it as if it has because God's purposes are so certain they can be spoken of in the past tense even when they are still future." For believers prone to anxiety about their standing — the chain is not fragile. None of its links depends on your performance. It is God's act from beginning to end.

συνεργεῖ From synergos (co-worker) — hence English "synergy." Present active indicative: ongoing, continuous action. God is always and actively causing things to work together. The prefix syn- (together) is crucial — individual things are not good in isolation; God coordinates them toward good. The all-encompassing panta (all things) has no exception clause.
προορίζω "Predestine/foreordain" — pro (before) + horizō (to mark out a boundary, determine). To mark out beforehand. Used in Acts 4:28 for God's predetermined plan; Eph 1:5,11 for adoption and inheritance. Here: foreordained to be conformed to Christ's image — the goal is Christ-likeness, not merely salvation from hell.
πρωτότοκος "Firstborn" (v.29) — prōtos (first) + tiktō (to bear/give birth). In OT: the firstborn held priority, preeminence, double inheritance. Christ is "firstborn among many brothers" — not an only child but the eldest of a family. The phrase holds together his uniqueness (firstborn) and our inclusion (brothers). We are the "many" in his train.

α Greek Exegesis ▼
ὑπερνικῶμεν hypernikōmen Hapax legomenon — appears only here in the entire Bible. Hyper (over/above/beyond) + nikaō (to conquer/overcome). "We more than conquer," "we super-conquer," "we are hyper-victorious." The victory is not merely sufficient but overwhelming. And critically: "through him who loved us" — the source is external to us, in Christ's love.
ἴδιον υἱόν "His own Son" (v.32) — idios emphasizes the uniqueness and intimate belonging. Not a son in the adopted sense but the proper, own Son. "He who did not spare his own Son" — the verb pheidomai (to spare) echoes Gen 22:16 (Abraham not sparing Isaac). The greater sacrifice guarantees the lesser gifts.
χωρίσει "Shall separate" (v.35) — chōrizō: to separate, divide, put apart. Paul lists eight potential separators: tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, sword, and in vv.38-39 a cosmic catalogue. Then the answer: nothing — the list of separators is exhaustive and the answer still holds.
ἀγάπη τοῦ Θεοῦ "The love of God" (v.39) — the final, bedrock anchor. Not our love for God (which could fail) but God's love for us (which cannot). Paul closes the entire argument of Romans 1-8 here: we are in the arms of a love that is not conditioned on our performance, that no cosmic force can breach, that is — as OLSHAUSEN put it — everlasting Love.

JFB on v.31: "What shall we then say to these things?" — the apostle pauses to let the full weight of Romans 6–8 register. "If God be for us, who can be against us?" — the form is a rhetorical question inviting the answer: no one with any ultimate effect. God's being "for us" encompasses all He has done in justification, sanctification, adoption, and glorification.

On v.32: "He that spared not his own Son" — JFB notes this is "a mysterious act of Self-sacrifice" — the language echoes the Akedah (Gen 22:16). Nothing can be withheld after the Son was not withheld.

On v.33–34: Who shall lay any charge? Who shall condemn? On v.35–36: Paul lists seven afflictions to show that none of them separates from Christ's love. 📚 Word Study ▼
ὑπερνικῶμεν Biblical hapax — only here. Hyper-nikaō: the intensifying hyper takes the already-strong nikaō (to conquer — same root as Nike, goddess of victory) and elevates it beyond ordinary conquering. "Super-conquerors," "overwhelmingly victorious." The present tense indicates ongoing status, not a one-time event. The means: "dia tou agapēsantos hēmas" — through the one who loved us (aorist: definite, historical act of love at the cross).
χωρίζω "To separate, divide" — used for geographical separation (Acts 18:1-2), marital separation (1 Cor 7:10-11), and here for the impossible cosmic separation from God's love. The word implies a clean, decisive division. Paul asks: can anything do this? Then answers with the exhaustive list — and the final denial. The love of God in Christ Jesus is inseparable from those who are in Him.
ἀγάπη The climactic word of Romans 8. Agapē — the love that chooses and commits regardless of the beloved's merit. The genitive in v.39 is "the love of God" — God's love directed toward us, not our love toward God (which could be interrupted). This love was demonstrated historically at the cross (v.32: "He did not spare His own Son") and declared cosmically: nothing in all creation can sever it.
